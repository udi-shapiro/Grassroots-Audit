      Abstract
Formal models for concurrent and distributed systems describe machines; the people who operate
them are either ignored or treated as external environment. Yet, key distributed systems—notably
grassroots platforms—include people operating their personal machines (smartphones), and their
faithful description must include the states of both people and machines and how they jointly effect
system behaviour.
    Here, we propose volition-guarded multiagent atomic transactions—executed atomically by
machines and guarded by their people’s volitions—as a mathematical foundation for specifying
systems consisting of people operating machines. Each agent’s state consists of a volitional state
and machine state; a transaction is enabled when the machine precondition holds and the guarding
persons are willing. For example, befriending two people is guarded by both; unfriending, by either;
a swap of coins is guarded by both parties, while a payment is guarded by the payer.
    We develop the mathematical machinery to express safety and liveness of platforms specified in
this framework, and provide example specifications of two grassroots platforms: social networks, and
currencies. These specifications are then used by AI to derive working implementations. We employ
here a simpler definition of ‘grassroots’ that better captures the informal notion—multiple instances
can form and operate independently, yet may coalesce—show that the platforms specified here are
grassroots under the new definition, and prove that two instances of a grassroots protocol can always
coalesce. We further introduce volitionally grassroots protocols, in which two groups can become
connected only by mutual consent—the first transaction coupling them must be willed by a member
of each—and an agent may leave, never again guarding a transaction, by which a platform records a
person’s death or incapacity and an AI agent’s retirement, two groups each holding an agent that
has not left being always able to connect; and show that both platforms are volitionally grassroots.

     1     Introduction

    The gap. Formal models for concurrent and distributed systems describe machines; the
    people who operate them are either ignored or treated as external environment (see Section 5).
    Across all surveyed formal traditions—Turing’s choice machines [52], CSP [22], CCS [34], I/O
    automata [32], angelic/demonic nondeterminism [6], ATL [3], game semantics [1], ceremony
    analysis [18], electronic institutions [19]—the person is modelled as environment, opponent,
    error source, or unconstrained nondeterministic process, but always as an entity external to
    the agent, never as a formal component of the agent’s state.
    Proposal. Here, we propose volition-guarded multiagent atomic transactions (henceforth,
    volition-guarded transactions)—executed atomically by machines and guarded by their
    people’s volitions—as a formal foundation for specifying systems consisting of people operating
    machines. Each agent’s state decomposes into a machine state and a volitional state; a
    transaction is enabled when the machine precondition holds and the guarding persons are
    willing. Volitions are thus persistent, inspectable agent state rather than point-of-choice
    nondeterminism, environment, or opponent moves as in prior formal traditions.
    Example: Grassroots social graphs. Each agent p maintains a set of friends cp ⊆ P .
    The social graph evolves via befriending and unfriending, specified as volition-guarded
    transactions:

         1. Befriend: c′p := cp ∪ {q}, c′q := cq ∪ {p}, provided q ∈

            {p, q}.
         2. Unfriend: c′p := cp \ {q}, c′q := cq \ {p}, provided q ∈ cp and p ∈ cq . Guarded by

    Befriending requires both persons to be willing; unfriending can be initiated by either. The
    guard determines which persons must be willing for a machine transaction to proceed. This
    distinction is the essence of volition-guarded transactions. A further example, child-safe
    social networking, appears in Appendix A.
    Motivating domain: grassroots platforms. Volition-guarded transactions are a general
    framework for systems of people operating machines. An example of a class of such systems,
    which motivated this work, is grassroots platforms [41, 42, 44, 49], which aim to offer an
    egalitarian alternative to global platforms, centralized and decentralized alike. Grassroots
    platforms consist of people operating their personal machines (smartphones), and can
    have multiple instances that emerge and operate independently of each other and of any
    global resource except the network, yet can interoperate and coalesce once interconnected.
    Key grassroots platforms include grassroots social networks [42, 48], grassroots coins and
    bonds [28, 44], and grassroots democratic communities [20, 49, 23]. No platform that operates
    on a shared global resource—a replicated ledger (Blockchain [36]), a distributed data structure
    (IPFS [7], DHT [33]), or a distributed pub/sub system with a global directory [13, 14, 11])—is
    grassroots. Mastodon [37] is peer-to-peer among servers, not people; BitTorrent [51] requires
    reaching a tracker or DHT boot nodes to join a swarm; Scuttlebutt [50, 24] is grassroots in
    design even if not formally proven as such.
        An earlier paper [45] introduced multiagent atomic transactions for grassroots platforms
    and presented transactions-based specifications of social graphs, cryptocurrencies, and
    democratic federations; that work did not address the role of people, transaction equivalence,
    or liveness, and employed the original definition of grassroots protocols.

Framework overview. We develop the mathematical machinery needed to express volition-
guarded transactions and the safety and liveness of grassroots platforms they specify. We
introduce transaction equivalence classes, which provide a natural notion of “same action”
across configurations, and define liveness in terms of such classes: a run is live iff every
persistently enabled class is eventually taken. We demonstrate the framework with two
grassroots platforms:
 1. Grassroots social networks [42, 48] let people maintain their friendship connections
    through local storage and peer-to-peer relationships without central control, with the
    social graph evolving through befriending and unfriending.
 2. Grassroots currencies [28, 44] let each person mint their own coins, backed by the
    goods and services they offer, and exchange them with others via atomic swaps; bonds
    extend coins with a maturity date, enabling interest-bearing credit, loans, and the full
    gamut of financial instruments, and we specify the coins.
The specifications presented here have been used by AI to derive working implementations
in GLP, a grassroots multiagent concurrent logic programming language [43], as reported
in [46, 48, 44, 47]. We employ here a simpler definition of grassroots protocols than the
original [41, 45], based on interleavings of correct runs, which better captures the informal
notion of grassroots, show that the platforms specified here are grassroots under the new
definition, and prove that two instances of a grassroots protocol can always coalesce. We
define volitionally grassroots protocols, in which two groups can become connected only by
the mutual consent of a member of each—the first transaction coupling them is guarded
on both sides—and in which an agent may leave, never again guarding a transaction, by
which a platform records a person’s death or incapacity and an AI agent’s retirement, two
groups each holding an agent that has not left being always able to connect; and prove both
platforms volitionally grassroots. We also prove that Bitcoin is not grassroots and show that
the argument applies to distributed hash tables and IPFS.
Use of AI tools. AI tools were used in drafting and revising the text of this paper. The
working implementations derived by AI from the specifications presented here are reported
in Appendix E.

 2     Volitional Multiagent Transition Systems
Earlier work introduced multiagent transition systems [39], grassroots protocols and plat-
forms [41], and their definition via multiagent atomic transactions [45]—capturing the
4   Describing People and their Machines


    behaviour of machines but not of the people operating them. Here we develop the mathemat-
    ical machinery to describe agents consisting of a person and a machine, and the volitional
    transactions they execute.
        A volitional transaction is a “regular” multiagent atomic transaction—henceforth, a
    machine transaction—guarded by the volitions of some, all, or none of the people whose
    machines participate; a person’s volitional state is a set of equivalence classes of machine
    transactions they are willing their machine to participate in. The social graph illustrates
    the two extremes: befriending is guarded by both p and q (the class of the ‘befriend p
    and q’ machine transaction must be in both volitions), while unfriending is guarded by
    either. A person may freely change their volitional state via volition changes; additionally, an
    equivalence class is removed from every agent’s volitional state when a machine transaction
    in this class is taken, fulfilling the will.
        The definitions of volitional transactions and agent states are mutually recursive: a
    volitional transaction is a machine transaction guarded by volitions, which are themselves
    sets of equivalence classes of machine transactions. We resolve this circularity bottom-up:
    first machine transactions, then their equivalence, then agent states (including volitional
    states), and finally volitional transactions.

    2.1    Agents, People, Machines, Volition-Guarded Transactions and
           Volitional Transactions
    We assume a countable, potentially infinite set of agents Π, but consider only finite subsets
    of it, so when referring to a particular set of agents P ⊂ Π we assume P to be nonempty and
    finite. We use ⊂ to denote the strict subset relation and ⊆ when equality is also possible,
    and use p ̸= q ∈ P as a shorthand for p ∈ P ∧ q ∈ P ∧ p ̸= q. As standard, we use S P to
    denote the set of all total functions from P to S, and if c ∈ S P we use cp (instead of c(p)) to
    denote the value of c at p ∈ P .
    ▶ Definition 1 (Machine State, Configuration, Transaction, Volition-Guarded Multiagent Atomic
    Transaction). Given an arbitrary set S of machine states, with a designated initial state
    s0 ∈ S, and agents Q ⊂ Π, a machine configuration over Q is a member of S Q , and a
    machine transaction over participants Q is a pair c → c′ ∈ (S Q )2 such that c ̸= c′ ; it is
    unary if |Q| = 1. Given such a machine transaction t, a volition-guarded multiagent
    atomic transaction over t—henceforth, volition-guarded transaction—is a pair (t, Q′ )
    where Q′ ⊆ Q are its guards.
        Machine transactions are atomic and asynchronous [39]—they can be carried out by their
    participants at any time, regardless of the states of non-participants. Participants include
    both active agents (whose state changes) and stationary agents (whose state is a precondition
    but does not change). Volition-guarded transactions are machine transactions that can be
    carried out only if their guards Q′ ⊆ Q are willing. Volition-guarded transactions do not
    distinguish between agents that initiate a transaction and those willing to participate in it.
    When we say a transaction is “guarded by {p, q},” both must be willing; when we say it is
    “guarded by either p or q,” we mean there are two volition-guarded transactions over the
    same machine transaction, (t, {p}) and (t, {q}), so that either person’s volition suffices.
        Distinct machine transactions can represent “the same action” in different configurations;
    we capture this with an equivalence relation on machine transactions.
    ▶ Definition 2 (Transaction Equivalence). Given a set of machine transactions R, a transac-
    tion equivalence is an equivalence relation ∼ on R such that t ∼ t′ implies t and t′ have the
    same participants, and R/∼ is countable. We write [t] for the equivalence class of t under ∼.

     3     Grassroots Protocols

    Here we define what a protocol is; define when a protocol is grassroots, using the notion of
    interleaving of correct runs; show how to define a protocol via a set of transactions; prove
    that any transactions-based protocol is oblivious under a natural condition; conclude that if
    it is also interactive, it is grassroots; and show that two instances of a grassroots protocol
    can always coalesce. For volitional transactions-based protocols, guarding every transaction
    of two or more participants guarantees this condition; and the volitional notion, volitionally
    grassroots, requires the first interaction between any two groups to be willed by a member of
    each, and interactivity only of groups each holding an agent that has not left. Proofs omitted
    from the body are in Appendix B.

3.1    Protocols and Grassroots Protocols
A protocol is a family of multiagent transition systems, one for each set of agents P ⊂ Π,
which share an underlying set of local machine states S with a designated initial state s0 . A
local-states function maps every set of agents P ⊂ Π to an arbitrary set of local machine
states S(P ) ⊂ S that includes s0 and satisfies P ⊂ P ′ ⊂ Π =⇒ S(P ) ⊂ S(P ′ ).

▶ Definition 13 (Protocol). A protocol F over a local-states function S is a family of multia-
gent transition systems that has exactly one transition system F(P ) = (C(P ), c0 (P ), T (P ), ∼ (P ))
for every P ⊂ Π, with agent states A(P ), configurations C(P ) := A(P )P , initial configura-
tion c0 (P ) ∈ C(P ), and partial equivalence ∼ (P ) on T (P ) determined by the protocol, such
that P ⊆ P ′ ⊂ Π implies A(P ) ⊆ A(P ′ ) and c0 (P )p = c0 (P ′ )p for every p ∈ P .

   Informally, in a grassroots protocol two disjoint groups of agents can each operate
independently—their interleaved correct runs are correct runs of the combined system—yet
the combined system offers genuinely new behaviours that neither group could produce on its
own. To capture this notion formally, we first define the interleaving of runs of two disjoint
groups.

▶ Definition 14 (Interleaving). Let P, P ′ ⊂ Π be disjoint nonempty sets of agents, r =
c0 , c1 , . . . a run of F(P ), and r′ = d0 , d1 , . . . a run of F(P ′ ). An interleaving of r and r′
is a sequence e0 , e1 , . . . of configurations in C(P ∪ P ′ ) for which there exist non-decreasing
sequences of indices (ik )k≥0 and (jk )k≥0 with i0 = j0 = 0 such that for every k ≥ 0:
 1. (ek )p = (cik )p for every p ∈ P ,
 2. (ek )q = (djk )q for every q ∈ P ′ ,
 3. if ek+1 exists, then exactly one of: (i) ik+1 = ik + 1 and jk+1 = jk (a P -step), or (ii)
     ik+1 = ik and jk+1 = jk + 1 (a P ′ -step).
Moreover, if r is finite of length n then ik = n for some k, and if r is infinite then for every
m ≥ 0 there is a k with ik = m; likewise for r′ and (jk ).

Note that an interleaving is well-defined: by Definition 13, A(P ) ⊆ A(P ∪ P ′ ) and A(P ′ ) ⊆
A(P ∪ P ′ ), so each ek , with p-components in A(P ) and q-components in A(P ′ ), is a valid
                                                   ′
configuration in C(P ∪ P ′ ) = A(P ∪ P ′ )P ∪P . Also, e0 = c0 (P ∪ P ′ ), since (e0 )p = (c0 )p =
c0 (P ∪ P ′ )p for p ∈ P and (e0 )q = (d0 )q = c0 (P ∪ P ′ )q for q ∈ P ′ , by the agreement of initial
configurations across F(P ), F(P ′ ), and F(P ∪ P ′ ).

▶ Definition 15 (Interaction, Interactive Run, First Interaction). Let Q ⊂ Π. For a configuration
c over Q and R ⊆ Q, write c|R for the restriction of c to R. A transition e → e′ of F(Q) is
an interaction between x =      ̸ y ∈ Q if e|{x} ̸= e′ |{x} and e|{y} ̸= e′ |{y} , and e|{x} → e′ |{x}
is not a transition of F({x}) or e|{y} → e′ |{y} is not a transition of F({y}). It is an
interaction between disjoint nonempty P, P ′ ⊆ Q if it is an interaction between some
x ∈ P and some y ∈ P ′ . A run r̂ of F(Q) is interactive between P and P ′ if some
transition of r̂ is an interaction between them; the first interaction of P and P ′ in r̂ is the
first such transition.

    We can now define the key notion of this paper, a grassroots protocol. The following
definition improves upon the original definition [41], which was formulated in terms of a
subset relation (P ⊂ P ′ ) and conditions on the availability of transitions, and upon the
definition of [45], which did not incorporate liveness. The new definition captures the informal
notion of grassroots directly, using disjoint groups and the interleaving of their correct runs;
the differences are discussed below.
10   Describing People and their Machines


     ▶ Definition 16 (Oblivious, Interactive, Grassroots). A protocol F is:
     1. oblivious if for every disjoint nonempty P, P ′ ⊂ Π, every interleaving of a correct run
        of F(P ) and a correct run of F(P ′ ) is a correct run of F(P ∪ P ′ ).
     2. interactive if for every Q ⊂ Π and disjoint nonempty P, P ′ ⊆ Q, every prefix of a correct
        run of F(Q) containing no interaction between P and P ′ is a prefix of a correct run of
        F(Q) that contains one.
     3. grassroots if it is oblivious and interactive.

     Oblivious. Being oblivious means two disjoint groups coexist without interference: any
     interleaving of a correct run of one with a correct run of the other is a correct run of the
     combined system, so each group’s correct operation is undisturbed by the other’s presence.
     Interactive. Being interactive means two groups that have not yet interacted can still do so:
     the combined system has a correct continuation in which a single transaction engages agents
     of both groups—a coupling that no interleaving of the groups’ separate runs can produce.
     The condition is required at every such point and not at the initial configuration alone, so a
     protocol whose groups can reach a state from which they can never couple is not interactive.
     The first such transaction in a run is its first interaction (Definition 15). The substance of
     interactivity is the content of these transactions, which the platforms in Section 4 illustrate.
     Coalescence. Agents that have taken part in a common interaction are joined; an instance
     is a maximal set of agents joined directly or indirectly, and two instances coalesce when they
     come to be one.

     ▶ Definition 17 (Interaction Graph, Instance, Coalescence). Let F be a protocol, Q ⊂ Π, and r
     a prefix of a run of F(Q). The interaction graph of r has vertices Q and an edge between
     a≠ b ∈ Q whenever some transition of r is an interaction between a and b. A nonempty
     P ⊆ Q is an instance in r if it is a connected component of the interaction graph of r.
     Disjoint nonempty P, P ′ ⊆ Q coalesce in a run r̂ of F(Q) if they are instances in some
     prefix of r̂ and lie in one component of the interaction graph of a longer prefix of r̂.

     ▶ Proposition 18 (Coalescence). Let F be a grassroots protocol, Q ⊂ Π, and P, P ′ ⊆ Q
     disjoint instances in a prefix r of a correct run of F(Q). Then r is a prefix of a correct run
     of F(Q) in which P and P ′ coalesce.

     Proof. Take p ∈ P and q ∈ P ′ . As P and P ′ are distinct components of the interaction
     graph of r, no transition of r is an interaction between p and q. By interactivity, r is a prefix
     of a correct run of F(Q) some transition of which is such an interaction; the interaction
     graph of that run has the edge between p and q, so P and P ′ lie in one of its components. ◀

         In Appendix C we show that federated systems (Mastodon) are not interactive, and that
     systems employing a shared global data structure—Bitcoin, distributed hash tables, IPFS,
     trackerless BitTorrent—are not oblivious, hence none of these is grassroots.

     3.2    Transactions-Based Grassroots Protocols
     A protocol can be specified by a set of machine transactions over a local-states function,
     lifting the closure construction of Section 2 from a fixed set of agents to the whole family.
     We show that such a protocol is oblivious under a natural condition, and hence grassroots
     when it is also interactive.

     ▶ Definition 19 (Transactions Over a Local-State Function). Let S be a local-states function.
     A set of transactions R is over S if every transaction t ∈ R is a multiagent transition over
Lewis-Pye and Shapiro                                                                               11


Q and S(P ′ ) for some Q ⊆ P ′ ⊂ Π. Given such a set R and P ⊂ Π, R(P ) := {t ∈ R :
t is over Q and S(P ′ ), Q ⊆ P ′ ⊆ P }.
    A transaction of agents of P from local states of P may reach a local state of a larger set
of agents, as when p adds to its state an agent outside P . Such a transaction is in R(P ∪ P ′ )
and not in R(P ), so an interleaving of correct runs of F(P ) and F(P ′ ) may owe it a liveness
obligation that the run of F(P ) does not owe. We exclude it.
▶ Definition 20 (Contained). A set of transactions R over a local-states function S is
contained if for every P ⊂ Π, every t = c → c′ in R with participants Q ⊆ P and c ∈ S(P )Q
is in R(P ).
For a set of volition-guarded transactions, a volition-guarded transaction is taken by its
machine transaction and a volition-guarded if-then-else transaction s → s′ ; s′′ guarded by p
by its two branches, with source s.
▶ Definition 21 (Transactions-Based Protocol). Let S be a local-states function and R a set
of machine transactions over S, in which distinct transactions have disjoint P -closures
for every P ⊂ Π, with transaction equivalence ∼ (Definition 2). The transactions-
based protocol over R, S, and ∼ assigns to each P ⊂ Π the multiagent transition
system F(P ) := (S(P )P , {s0 }P , R(P )↑P, ∼↑P ), where R(P )↑P and ∼↑P are the P -closures
(Definition 10) of R(P ) and of ∼ restricted to R(P ). In particular A(P ) = S(P ), monotone
as Definition 13 requires, C(P ) = S(P )P , and c0 (P ) = {s0 }P .
    By Definition 21, the transitions of F(P ∪ P ′ ) are induced by the machine transactions
in R(P ∪ P ′ ). Since liveness ranges over the equivalence classes of F(P ∪ P ′ ) (Definition 7),
any class [t] in R(P ∪ P ′ )/ ∼ whose transactions have participants spanning both P and P ′
could obstruct obliviousness if enabled in an interleaving; so could a class whose transactions
have participants in P and that is not a class of F(P ), which containment excludes. The
following proposition identifies the conditions under which neither occurs.
▶ Proposition 22. A transactions-based protocol over a contained set of transactions is
oblivious provided that for every disjoint nonempty P, P ′ ⊂ Π, no equivalence class whose
transactions have participants spanning both P and P ′ is ever enabled in any interleaving of
correct runs of F(P ) and F(P ′ ).

3.3    Volitional Transactions-Based Grassroots Protocols
A volitional transactions-based protocol assigns to each set of agents the volitional multiagent
transition system induced by its volition-guarded transactions. Obliviousness follows from
a syntactic guard condition, in three steps. First, the Volitional Containment Lemma
(Lemma 24) establishes that an agent’s volitional state, in any configuration of F(P ), never
contains classes of transactions outside R(P ) (Definition 19). Second, Proposition 26 uses
this invariant to reduce obliviousness to a condition on equivalence classes in the interleaving.
Third, Corollary 27 establishes that condition whenever the set of transactions is contained
and every volition-guarded transaction of two or more participants has a nonempty guard. A
protocol so oblivious is grassroots once it is interactive (Definition 16). We then define the
volitional notion: volitionally grassroots requires obliviousness, interactivity of groups each
holding an agent that has not left, and the first interaction between any two groups willed
by a member of each.
    Throughout this subsection, ∼ is an equivalence on TR , the machine transactions underly-
ing the whole set R; for T ′ ⊆ TR we write T ′ /∼ for the set of ∼-classes with a representative
in T ′ , and [t] for the class of t in TR /∼. Hence P ⊆ P ′ implies TR(P ) /∼ ⊆ TR(P ′ ) /∼.

     ▶ Definition 23 (Volitional Transactions-Based Protocol). Let S be a local-states function and
     R a set of volition-guarded transactions over S with equivalence ∼. The protocol F over
     R, S, and ∼ assigns to each set of agents P ⊂ Π the volitional multiagent transition system
     F(P ) induced by (S(P ), R(P ), ∼) over P (Definition 11). In particular, C(P ) = A(P )P with
     agent state space A(P ) := 2TR(P ) /∼ × S(P )—monotone in P , as Definition 13 requires—and
     c0 (P ) has p-component (∅, s0 ) for every p ∈ P .
        The obliviousness results below rest on the following invariant: in a group’s own system
     an agent wills only transactions internal to the group, so the guard of a transaction that
     reaches another group cannot will it while the group runs alone, and the transaction is never
     enabled in an interleaving.

     over a set of volition-guarded transactions R with equivalence ∼. For every P ⊂ Π, every

     R(P ) for some Q′ }.
     Proof. By Definition 11, every configuration of F(P ) is in A(P )P with A(P ) = 2TR(P ) /∼ ×
     S(P ), so cvp ⊆ TR(P ) / ∼ for every p ∈ P .                                               ◀

     P, P ′ ⊂ Π disjoint and nonempty. Every finite prefix of an interleaving of a correct run of
     F(P ) and a correct run of F(P ′ ) is a finite safe run of F(P ∪ P ′ ).
     ▶ Proposition 26. A volitional transactions-based protocol over a contained set of transactions
     is oblivious provided that for every disjoint nonempty P, P ′ ⊂ Π, no equivalence class whose
     transactions have participants spanning both P and P ′ is ever enabled in any interleaving of
     correct runs of F(P ) and F(P ′ ).
        When every volition-guarded transaction of two or more participants has a nonempty
     guard, the hypothesis of Proposition 26 is established by a uniform argument about volitions,
     without invoking platform-specific machine preconditions.
     ▶ Corollary 27 (Guarded Obliviousness). A volitional transactions-based protocol over a con-
     tained set of volition-guarded transactions R is oblivious if every volition-guarded transaction
     (t, Q′ ) ∈ R whose machine transaction t has two or more participants has a nonempty guard,
     Q′ ̸= ∅.
         Corollary 27 reduces obliviousness—for volitional transactions-based protocols—to con-
     tainment and a syntactic check on guards. The two platforms of Section 4 apply it uniformly.
         Containment is needed. Let S(P ) := 2P , and let an unguarded unary transaction of
     p add to its state an agent it does not hold, the additions of q by p forming one class. A
     correct run of F({p}) ends with p holding {p}, and one of F({q}) with q holding {q}. Their
     interleaving is not a correct run of F({p, q}), in which p adding q is enabled from some point
     on and never taken. Every transaction is unary, so the guard condition holds; the set is
     not contained, since p adding q has its source in S({p}) and is not in R({p}). Guarding the
     additions by their agent does not help where all additions by p form one class: p wills the
     class again after adding itself, and in F({p}) the class is then never enabled.
         An agent has left when no transaction it guards can again be machine-enabled, so that
     its will never again has effect. Leaving is a machine condition: a platform records a person’s
     death or incapacity, and the retirement of an AI agent operating a machine, by a machine
     state from which no transaction the agent guards is machine-enabled. We define leaving, and
     require interactivity only of groups each holding an agent that has not left.

▶ Definition 29 (Interactive Among the Agents That Have Not Left). A volitional transactions-

An interactive protocol is interactive among the agents that have not left, the latter requiring
a continuation of fewer prefixes. A group all of whose agents have left can never be connected
by an act one of them wills, and the definition asks nothing of it.
    In a run of F(P ∪ P ′ ) a member may will a transaction reaching the other group, which
a group’s own run forbids; the volitional notion constrains the resulting coupling:

▶ Definition 30 (Volitionally Grassroots). A volitional transactions-based protocol F is
volitionally grassroots if it is oblivious, interactive among the agents that have not left,
and, for every disjoint nonempty P, P ′ ⊂ Π and every safe run of F(P ∪ P ′ ) interactive
between P and P ′ , the first interaction of P and P ′ is induced by a volition-guarded transaction
(t, Q′ ) with Q′ ∩ P ̸= ∅ and Q′ ∩ P ′ ̸= ∅.

Two groups thus become connected only by an act a person of each wills, and a grassroots
protocol meeting the condition on first interactions is volitionally grassroots. An else
transaction (Definition 6) is over a single participant and so is never an interaction; a binary
transaction with an else-branch would let an agent of another group, who never wills it,
trigger it, and could be a first interaction willed by neither, which is why Definition 5 admits
only unary ones.

▶ Corollary 31 (Volitional Coalescence). Let F be a volitionally grassroots protocol, Q ⊂ Π,
and P, P ′ ⊆ Q disjoint instances in a prefix r of a correct run of F(Q), each holding an
agent that has not left in the last configuration of r. Then r is a prefix of a correct run of
F(Q) in which P and P ′ coalesce.

Proof. As P and P ′ are distinct components of the interaction graph of r, no transition
of r is an interaction between them, and each holds an agent that has not left in the last
configuration of r; by Definition 29, r is a prefix of a correct run of F(Q) some transition
of which is an interaction between an agent of P and one of P ′ , so P and P ′ lie in one
component of the interaction graph of that run.                                            ◀


 4     Grassroots Platforms via Volition-Guarded Transactions
We now present two grassroots platforms: grassroots social networks and grassroots currencies.
For each platform, we specify its volition-guarded transactions, define the induced volitional
multiagent transition system, prove platform-specific invariants, and prove the platform is
grassroots via Corollary 27 and Definition 16. By invariants we mean properties preserved
across all runs of the induced multiagent transition system; these are the platform-specific
analogue of safety in the classical sense. In both platforms every transaction is guarded, so
the condition of Definition 11 on the closures of unguarded transactions holds. Proofs are in
Appendix B.

     4.1    Grassroots Social Networks via Befriending and Unfriending
     In a grassroots social network [42], the social graph is stored distributively under the control
     of the people themselves, with each person storing the local neighbourhood pertaining to
     them, and no third party having access unless explicitly granted. The original definition [42]
     was via a unary multiagent transition system; here both actions are specified as binary
     transactions.
         Each agent p maintains, as its local state, a finite set cp ⊆ P recording the friends
     of p; initially cp = ∅. Befriending adds q to cp and p to cq ; unfriending removes them.
     Communication functions of a social network can be added, under the restriction that
     communication occurs only among friends [42]. A liveness theorem can be proven for this
     design [42], stating that if a person p who follows a person q is connected to q via a chain of
     mutual friends, each of them correct and following q, then p will eventually receive every item
     on q’s feed. The specification of the grassroots social graph is the foundation for grassroots
     social networks with feeds, groups, messaging, explored elsewhere [42, 43, 48].
     ▶ Definition 32 (Grassroots Social Graph). The grassroots social graph SG is the protocol
     over the befriend and unfriend volition-guarded transactions of the Introduction, with local-
     states function S(P ) := 2P and equivalence ∼ that identifies all befriend transactions of the
     same pair {p, q} with each other and all unfriend transactions of the same pair with each
     other, per Definition 23.

     Invariants.
     ▶ Lemma 33 (Mutuality). Given a safe run r of SG, a configuration c ∈ r, and agents
     p, q ∈ P , q ∈ cp ⇐⇒ p ∈ cq .
         We note that each configuration c in a safe run r of SG induces a graph with agents as
     vertices and an edge p ↔ q when q ∈ cp (equivalently, by Lemma 33, when p ∈ cq ), and that
     the graphs induced by two consecutive configurations in r differ by exactly one added or
     removed edge.
     Transaction equivalence. All befriend transactions of the same pair {p, q}—differing in
     the configurations in which they occur—form an equivalence class. Similarly for unfriend.
     Liveness. Liveness applies to the befriend and unfriend classes (Definition 7). Unfriending is
     guarded by either p or q: once either person wills the class, the transaction becomes enabled
     and must eventually be taken. Befriending is guarded by both p and q: it becomes enabled
     only when both persons will the class, and must then eventually be taken.
     Grassroots.
     ▶ Corollary 34. The grassroots social graph SG is volitionally grassroots.

     4.2    Grassroots Currencies
     Grassroots coins [28, 44] are units of debt that can be issued and traded digitally by any
     person. Each person’s coins are backed by the goods and services they offer, priced in
     their own currency, with liquidity arising from mutual credit via coin exchange among
     persons that know and trust each other. Grassroots bonds [44] extend grassroots coins with a
     maturity date, so that a coin can be lent for an interest-bearing bond; digital social contracts—
     voluntary agreements among persons, specified, fulfilled, and enforced digitally—can then
     express the full gamut of financial instruments as the voluntary swap of bonds, including
     credit lines, loans, sale of debt, forward contracts, options, and escrow-based instruments [44].
     Here we specify the coins.

      5     Related Work

     Atomic transactions. This work extends the notion of multiagent atomic transactions
     of [45] with volitions. Atomic transactions were investigated early in distributed computing,
     mostly in the context of database systems [26, 31, 29]. Most research since then has focused on
     their efficient and robust implementation [10, 12]. The integration of atomic transactions in
     programming languages has also been explored [9]. In terms of formal models of concurrency,
     the extension of CCS with atomic transactions has been investigated in the past [2, 15, 16],
     but seemingly without follow-on research. While transition systems have been the bedrock
     of abstract models of computation since the Turing machine, we are not aware of previous
     attempts to explore atomic transactions within their context.
     Formal models of persons in concurrent systems. The formal methods tradition has a
     long lineage of modelling human agents as sources of nondeterminism alongside deterministic
     machines, but—to the best of our knowledge—without decomposing an agent into person and
     machine as components of its state. A detailed survey—covering Turing’s choice machines,
     CSP and CCS, I/O automata and reactive systems, angelic/demonic nondeterminism, game
     structures and alternating-time temporal logic, ceremony analysis, and normative multiagent
     systems and electronic institutions—appears in Appendix F.
         Across all traditions, the person is modelled as environment, opponent, error source, or
     unconstrained nondeterministic process—but always as an entity external to the agent, never
     as a formal component of the agent’s state. The present work, to the best of our knowledge,
     is the first to decompose each agent’s state into a machine state and a volitional state within
     a multiagent transition system, with machine transactions conditioned on the volitions of
     the agents’ persons, and with liveness arising from the interplay of personal volitions and
     machine obligations rather than from a designated set of live transitions.


      6     Conclusion and Future Work

     We have presented volition-guarded multiagent atomic transactions—a formal foundation
     for describing systems consisting of people operating machines—in which each agent’s state
     decomposes into a machine state and a volitional state, and machine transactions are guarded
     by their people’s volitions. We have developed the mathematical machinery needed to
     express the safety and liveness of grassroots platforms thus specified, and demonstrated the
     framework on two grassroots platforms: social networks and currencies. We have provided a
     simpler definition of grassroots that better captures the informal notion and excludes systems
     based on shared global data structures.
         The framework extends naturally to platforms with richer guard structures—such as
     grassroots federations, in which transactions are guarded by supermajorities or assemblies of
     community members rather than by all participants—deferred to future work.
         The original framework [39] also considered faulty computations and fault-tolerant imple-
     mentations. We have adapted its notions of implementation and fault-resilient implementation
     to the present setting; establishing fault-tolerant implementations of the specifications pre-
     sented here is left to future work.

 A      Further Examples of Grassroots Platforms Specified by
        Volition-Guarded Transactions
A grassroots platform is specified by a set of volition-guarded transactions over a local-states
function; the formal machinery for deriving a transition system and proving it grassroots
is introduced in Section 2. Here we present the volition-guarded transactions of a further
grassroots platform, illustrating the range of guard structures that arise in practice; the coins
and the social graph are specified in Section 4.
Child-safe social networks. A child-safe social network is a grassroots social network in
which a child’s partaking in online activities is subject to parental consent; its specification as
a digital social contract is work in preparation, and its implementation in vGLP is reported
in [48]. Each agent a maintains its friends ca ⊆ P , the agents ηa it knows, whether it is a
child χa ∈ {0, 1}, and the children κa ⊆ P of whom it is a parent; an agent is a child when
χa = 1 and a person otherwise, and a parent of r when r ∈ κa . How an agent comes to know
another, and a child to have a parent, is specified by the contract; components not named
are unchanged.

     1. Child befriend, over {r, s, p, q}, where r ̸= s, r ∈ κp , s ∈ κq , χr = χs = 1,
        χp = χq = 0, q ∈ cp or q = p, s ∈ ηr , r ∈ ηs , and s ∈    / cr : c′r := cr ∪ {s},
        c′s := cs ∪ {r}. Guarded by {r, s, p, q}.
     2. Child unfriend, over {r, s, p}, where r ∈ κp , χr = 1, χp = 0, s ∈ cr and r ∈/ κs :
        c′r := cr \ {s}, c′s := cs \ {r}. Guarded by p.

Two children who know each other become friends by an act of both and of a parent of each,
the two parents being friends or one parent; the parents’ states do not change. A parent of a
20   Describing People and their Machines


     child may end any friendship of the child but one with a parent of the child, and the two
     friends end it by Unfriend of the social graph.
     Summary. The guard captures the essential distinction between voluntary and obligatory
     transactions. Befriending and voluntary swaps are guarded by all participants—they require
     mutual willingness. Unfriending, payments, redemptions and child unfriending are guarded
     by a single party—they are obligatory once initiated. Child befriending illustrates the
     most complex case: a quaternary guard requiring all four agents to be willing, two of them
     stationary. Unguarded transactions (guard Q′ = ∅), such as group message delivery [48], are
     purely mechanical and require no volitions.
     Sybils. Sybils [17] arise when a person operates two or more identities. Defending against
     sybils is a broad research area [27, 4], which is largely algorithmic, often evaluated exper-
     imentally. A formal treatment of sybils requires a formal notion of a person and of the
     correspondence between persons and the identifiers they own [38]. The present framework
     offers a natural vehicle for formally studying sybils and sybil resilience: since an agent is a
     person operating a machine, identified by the public key of the keypair its machine holds, a
     sybil is a single person operating two or more agents under distinct keypairs, and absent
     sybils agents are in one-to-one correspondence with persons. Using the current framework to
     formally study sybil resilience is a subject of future work.


      B      Deferred Proofs
     A transaction and a transition are structurally identical—both are pairs of configurations—
     but differ in their role: a transaction is specified over its participants Q, the agents whose
     states are preconditions for the transaction to occur, and says nothing about agents outside
     Q; different transactions may have different sets of participants. A transition, by contrast,
     is over a fixed set of agents P , as it is a building block of a transition system over P
     that consists of transitions over P . Given a set of transactions, each over its own set of
     participants, the closure operator induces from them a set of transitions over a fixed P , in
     which non-participants remain stationary.
         A set of machine transactions R over S, each with participants Q ⊆ P , defines a multiagent
     transition system over S and P as follows:

     ▶ Definition 39 (Transactions-Based Multiagent Transition System). Given agents P ⊂ Π,
     states S with initial state s0 ∈ S, and a set of transactions R, each t ∈ R over some Q ⊆ P
     and S, the transactions-based multiagent transition system over P , S, and R is the
     multiagent transition system T S = (S P , {s0 }P , R↑P, ∅), with ∼ the empty partial equivalence,
     so that no transition belongs to a class and no liveness obligation is imposed.

     In other words, one can fully specify a multiagent transition system over S and P simply by
     providing a set of transactions over S, each with participants Q ⊆ P .
     Lemma 8 (Extension) (restated). Every finite safe run of a transition system is a prefix

a Swap of those lots and taking it; the swap changes the local states of both p and q and puts
q-coins in p’s holdings, which no local state of GC ({p}) carries, and is therefore an interaction
between p and q and hence between P and P ′ . The result is a finite safe run of GC (Q)
having r̂ as a prefix and containing an interaction between P and P ′ , and by Lemma 8 it is
a prefix of a correct run of GC (Q). Thus GC is oblivious and interactive, hence grassroots
by Definition 16, and interactive among the agents that have not left (Definition 29).
    Volitionally grassroots: By Definition 15, the first interaction of disjoint nonempty P, P ′
in any safe interactive run changes the state of an agent in each group; Mint changes the
24   Describing People and their Machines


     state of one agent, so in GC the first interaction is a Swap, Pay or Redeem between an agent
     of P and one of P ′ . A Pay or Redeem in which p ∈ P acts towards q ∈ P ′ requires p to hold
     a q-coin. By the proof of Conservation of Money (Lemma 37), every q-coin originates in a
     Mint by q, and every other transaction moves coins between its participants; so the first
     transaction that puts a q-coin at an agent x ∈ P moves it from an agent of P ′ , changes the
     states of both, and puts at x a coin no local state of GC ({x}) carries—itself an interaction.
     So the first interaction is neither a Pay nor a Redeem. It is therefore a Swap, guarded by
     both its participants, one in P and one in P ′ . Hence GC is volitionally grassroots.       ◀

     Relation to the original grassroots definition. The original definition of grassroots
     protocols [41, 45] used a subset relation (P ⊂ P ′ ) and conditions on transition availability,
     without incorporating liveness. The new definition uses disjoint groups and interleavings
     of correct runs. The two definitions are not comparable in general—neither implies the
     other—but we informally claim that, for the platforms considered here, both definitions
     agree.

      C      Systems Based on Shared Global Data Structures Are Not
             Grassroots
     Federated systems such as Mastodon [37] are oblivious, as servers in one group can ignore
     servers in the other, but are not interactive: a group of clients P without a server cannot do
     more when joined by another group of clients P ′ , also without a server. Any protocol that
     employs a shared global data structure—whether replicated (Blockchain [36]) or distributed
     (DHT [33], IPFS [7])—is not oblivious, and hence not grassroots; the argument covers
     trackerless BitTorrent [51], which relies on a DHT. Below we prove that Bitcoin is not
     grassroots and show that the same argument applies to distributed hash tables and IPFS.
         We model Bitcoin as a protocol (Definition 13) in which the local state of an agent is
     a set of blocks, initially the genesis block, and a set of agents it knows, initially the other
     members of a set B ⊂ Π of bootnodes for a member of B and empty otherwise; its transitions
     are the closures of two machine transactions: Mine, over {p}, which adds to p’s blocks a
     block named by p that p does not hold; and Propagate, over {p, q} with p known to q,
     which adds to q’s blocks a block p holds and q does not. The Propagates of one block to one
     agent form one class and a Mine is in no class, so in a correct run every block held by an
     agent is eventually held by every agent that knows it.

     ▶ Proposition 41. Bitcoin is not grassroots.

     Proof. We argue that Bitcoin is not oblivious. Consider disjoint P, P ′ ⊂ Π each containing a
     bootnode, q ∈ P and q ′ ∈ P ′ , a correct run of F(P ) in which q mines a block b, a correct run
     of F(P ′ ), and an interleaving e of the two. In e, q ′ knows q from the initial configuration,
     and from the step at which q mines b on, q holds b and q ′ does not, since every step of e is a
     P -step or a P ′ -step and the Propagate of b to q ′ is over {q, q ′ }. So the class of the Propagates
     of b to q ′ is enabled in every configuration of a suffix of e and no member of it is taken; e is
     not live, hence not a correct run of F(P ∪ P ′ ), and Bitcoin is not oblivious.                     ◀

     Distributed Hash Tables and IPFS. The same argument applies to systems based on
     distributed hash tables [33] and distributed file systems such as IPFS [7]. In a DHT, a
     lookup by a member of P may require routing through a member of P ′ , so that a lookup
     transition that succeeds when P runs alone does not correspond to a valid transition in the
     combined system where routing tables reflect both groups. More precisely: in an interleaving
Lewis-Pye and Shapiro                                                                                          25


of correct runs of P and P ′ , each group builds its own routing table over its own members.
A lookup in the combined system P ∪ P ′ must route through the combined table, but in
the interleaving no routing entry connecting the two groups is ever established, so lookups
that should succeed in P ∪ P ′ (by routing through P ′ ) instead fail or route incorrectly. The
liveness condition requiring that lookups for keys stored by correct agents eventually succeed
is thus violated. The argument for IPFS is analogous, as it relies on a DHT (specifically,
a Kademlia-based DHT [33]) for content discovery. Trackerless BitTorrent [51] relies on a
Kademlia-based DHT for peer discovery, so the argument applies to it as well.

 D       Implementations, Faults, and Resilience
An implementation relates a specification transition system to one that implements it, via a
mapping of states. We adapt the notion of implementation among transition systems [39, 46],
with safety and liveness as defined above.

▶ Definition 42 (Implementation). Given transition systems T S = (S, s0 , T, ∼), the spec-
ification, and T S ′ = (S ′ , s′0 , T ′ , ∼′ ), an implementation of T S by T S ′ is a function
σ : S ′ → S with σ(s′0 ) = s0 , in which case the pair (T S ′ , σ) is an implementation of T S.
Given a computation r′ = s′1 , s′2 , . . . of T S ′ , σ(r′ ) is the computation σ(s′1 ), σ(s′2 ), . . . with
consecutive repetitions removed; a transition s′ → d′ of T S ′ with σ(s′ ) = σ(d′ ) is a stutter,
contributing no step to σ(r′ ).

▶ Definition 43 (Correct and Complete Implementation). An implementation (T S ′ , σ) of T S
is correct if σ maps every correct run of T S ′ to a correct run of T S, and complete if every
correct run r of T S is σ(r′ ) for some correct run r′ of T S ′ .

   The original framework [39] also accounts for faulty behaviour and its resilience, which
we adapt to the present setting.

▶ Definition 44 (Safety Fault, Fault-Resilient Implementation). Let (T S ′ , σ) be an imple-
mentation of T S, with T S ′ = (S ′ , s′0 , T ′ , ∼′ ). A safety fault is a set F ⊆ S ′2 \ T ′ of
faulty transitions, and a computation performs F if it includes a transition in F . The
implementation is F -resilient if σ maps every live run r′ ⊆ T ′ ∪ F of T S ′ to a correct run
of T S.

Faulty transitions lie outside T ′ , hence in no class of ∼′ , so they impose no liveness require-
ment.
   The dual of a safety fault is a liveness fault: rather than acting incorrectly, correct
agents cease to act, so that obligations that ought to be met are not. A safety fault enlarges
the transitions that may occur; a liveness fault waives some of the obligations that liveness
would otherwise impose.

▶ Definition 45 (Liveness Fault, Liveness-Fault-Resilient Implementation). Let (T S ′ , σ) be an
implementation of T S, with T S ′ = (S ′ , s′0 , T ′ , ∼′ ). A liveness fault is a set Λ ⊆ T ′ /∼′
of liveness classes. A run performs Λ if it is not live with respect to some class in Λ. A
run is Λ-live if no class [t] ∈ T ′ /∼′ \ Λ is enabled in every state of some suffix with no
member of [t] occurring in the suffix—that is, live for ∼′ with the classes in Λ removed from
its domain. The implementation is Λ-resilient if σ maps every safe Λ-live run r′ ⊆ T ′ of
T S ′ to a correct run of T S.

With Λ = ∅ the Λ-live runs are exactly the live runs. In a volitional multiagent transition
system the canonical liveness fault is fail-stop: when a set of agents D ⊆ P cease to
26   Describing People and their Machines


     act, the waived classes are ΛD := {[t] ∈ T ′ /∼′ : some participant of t is in D}, every class
     some member of which requires a stopped agent. This is well-defined because ∼′ -equivalent
     transactions share participants (Definition 2); and a class is waived whichever of its guards
     is live, since every member of it has a stopped participant.
          The instance-level notion of resilient implementation that composes along a protocol
     stack is developed in [39] and can be adapted to this setting.


      E     Implementation
     The specifications presented here provide the formal foundation for grassroots platforms.
     Here, we discuss how these platforms can be implemented, focusing on the role of GLP as
     the implementation language.
     GLP as implementation language. Grassroots Logic Programs (GLP) [43] is designed for
     implementing grassroots platforms on networked smartphones. A multiagent implementation
     of GLP, madGLP, has been developed and proven to be a correct and outcome-complete
     implementation of maGLP, and the madGLP protocol has been proven grassroots [46].
     The abstraction cascade. A grassroots platform is specified as a digital social contract,
     whose clauses are written as act schemas, work in preparation; a compiler reads the act
     schemas and compiles them to vGLP [48], to GLP, and to the app the people use. A contract
     also carries pragmas, which name the screens of the app and the acts its buttons propose,
     and which do not affect the semantics of its acts. Volition-guarded transactions are a side
     branch of this cascade: the transactions of a contract are printed from its act schemas, and
     carry no pragma. The repository of GLP holds every contract, its act schemas, its pragmas
     and its transactions.
     AI-derived implementations. The mathematical foundations presented here and in related
     papers have been used by AI to derive working implementations: (i) a workstation-based
     implementation of concurrent GLP and a smartphone-based multiagent implementation
     of GLP, both in Dart, derived from the formal operational semantics [46]; (ii) a GLP
     implementation of the grassroots social graph, child-safe social networking with parental-
     consent-based befriending and group membership [48]; (iii) a GLP implementation of
     grassroots bonds, including a running six-agent village market scenario exercising symmetric
     and asymmetric credit, payments, redemption, escrow, and sale of debt [44]; and (iv) a
     moded type system for GLP, implemented in Dart from a mathematical specification [47].
     Enforcement. Enforcement of the digital social contract—ensuring that participants cannot
     deviate from the protocol as programmed—is achieved via mutual attestation among the
     participants’ machines, as described in work on secure GLP.


      F     Formal Models of Persons in Concurrent Systems
     The formal methods tradition has a long lineage of modelling human agents as sources
     of nondeterminism alongside deterministic machines, but—to the best of our knowledge—
     without decomposing an agent into person and machine as components of its state.
     Turing’s choice machines. Before defining what we now call Turing machines (automatic
     machines, or a-machines), Turing [52] introduced choice machines (c-machines), “whose
     motion is only partially determined by the configuration”—at designated states, the machine
     “cannot go on until some arbitrary choice has been made by an external operator.” The
     external operator is a person who freely chooses between alternatives; the sequence of choices
Lewis-Pye and Shapiro                                                                              27


determines which computation unfolds. Turing immediately set c-machines aside, showing
that any c-machine computation can be enumerated by an a-machine. His 1939 oracle
machines [53] extend this further: the oracle “cannot be a machine” and provides answers
the computation cannot derive internally. Both formalisms model the person as external to
the machine, providing input at designated points.
Process algebras. Hoare’s CSP [22] provides the cleanest process-algebraic encoding of
human choice. External choice (□) offers the environment—potentially a person—a selection
among initial events, while internal choice (⊓) is resolved by the system. However, the person
remains outside the system boundary: CSP models what the person does, not what the
person is willing to do. Milner’s CCS [34] uses a single summation operator without formally
separating internal from external nondeterminism at the syntactic level. In his later work on
bigraphs [35], Milner makes the scope explicit: agents “can be artificial, as in computing
systems. . . or they can be natural, e.g. communicating humans.” Both CSP and CCS model
agents uniformly—there is no formal distinction between a person and a machine within the
same agent.
I/O automata and reactive systems. Lynch and Tuttle’s I/O automata [32] partition
actions into input (environment-controlled), output (automaton-controlled), and internal
actions. The key property is input-enabling: an automaton cannot block input actions, so
the environment—potentially a human operator—can act at any moment. The Hybrid I/O
Automata extension [30] explicitly states that HIOAs are “intended to model all components
of hybrid systems, including. . . humans.” The person, however, is part of the environment,
not a component of the automaton’s state. Harel and Pnueli’s reactive systems paradigm [21]
draws the foundational dichotomy between transformational systems (batch, terminating)
and reactive systems (ongoing interaction with environment). The system is deterministic;
all nondeterminism is attributed to the environment. This paradigm was explicitly motivated
by human-machine interaction, yet the formalism treats the human as environment rather
than as a component of the system.
Angelic and demonic nondeterminism. The distinction between angelic and demonic
nondeterminism, developed by Back and von Wright [6] in the refinement calculus, provides
a semantic treatment relevant to the person/machine boundary. Demonic nondeterminism
models adversarial environments (the worst-case choice is made); angelic nondeterminism
models cooperative choices (the best-case choice is made). This duality is the closest precursor
to the volition/obligation distinction in the present work: a volitional transaction guarded
by both parties (both must be willing) versus one guarded by either party (either can
force it). Two distinctions separate the frameworks. First, the refinement calculus operates
within a sequential program framework, not a multiagent transition system. Second, angelic
nondeterminism is a point-of-choice semantics: a choice is resolved locally at each transition,
with no residue carried forward. Volitions, in contrast, are persistent, inspectable state that
accumulates across transitions; a guard condition reads an agent’s record of willing over the
history of the run, not a single local resolution. This shift—from choice as a point-semantic
primitive to choice as state—is what lets volitions be shared, compared, and reasoned about
within the transition system, rather than external to it.
Game structures and alternating-time temporal logic. Module checking [25] mod-
els open systems with the environment fully adversarial. Alternating-time temporal logic
(ATL) [3] interprets formulas over concurrent game structures where multiple agents simul-
taneously choose actions. Game semantics [1] models computation as dialogue between
Proponent (program, following a deterministic strategy) and Opponent (environment, making
free moves). These frameworks treat agents as symmetric players but do not decompose a
28   Describing People and their Machines


     single agent into person and machine components.
     Ceremony analysis and human-interactive verification. Ellison’s ceremony analy-
     sis [18] extends security protocol analysis to include human participants as protocol nodes.
     Bolton’s Enhanced Operator Function Model (EOFM) [8] translates hierarchical human
     task models into state machines for model checking, with the human as the sole source
     of nondeterminism. Both treat human nondeterminism as a source of error to be verified
     against, rather than as a source of legitimate volition to be formally recorded.
     Normative multiagent systems and electronic institutions. Electronic institutions [19]
     model multiagent interaction as dialogical frameworks where human and software agents
     are treated uniformly as role-playing entities. Normative multiagent systems [5] use Event
     Calculus to specify societies where agents “may fail to, or even choose not to, conform to the
     specifications.” These approaches model norms that constrain agents, but do not decompose
     an agent’s state into machine and volitional components, nor do they formalise the distinction
     between transactions requiring all parties to be willing and those that are obligatory once
     initiated.
