      Abstract. Grassroots Logic Programs (GLP) is a concurrent logic pro-
      gramming language in which logic variables are partitioned into paired
      readers and writers. An assignment is produced at most once via a writer
      and consumed at most once via its paired reader, and may contain addi-
      tional readers and/or writers. This enables the concise expression of rich
      multidirectional communication modalities.
      The language was introduced together with concurrent (cGLP) and
      multiagent (maGLP) operational semantics. Here, we derive from these
      (i) dGLP, a deterministic counterpart of cGLP, and (ii) madGLP,
      a counterpart of maGLP in which deterministic agents communicate
      solely by asynchronous message passing, and prove them correct against
      their abstract counterparts. maGLP shared variable pairs spanning
      agents can be implemented by two local variable pairs joined by a global
      link, with correctness following from disjoint substitution commutativity
      (a consequence of GLP’s single-occurrence invariant). We further prove
      that madGLP is grassroots. Both dGLP and madGLP serve as formal
      specifications for an AI-driven implementation discipline (math →
      informal spec → Dart) employed and described here: from dGLP, AI
      (Claude) developed a workstation-based GLP implementation in Dart,
      and from madGLP it is developing a smartphone-based multiagent one.

      Keywords: concurrent logic programming · multiagent systems · transi-
      tion systems · operational semantics · peer-to-peer · grassroots platforms


1    Introduction
Grassroots platforms. A digital platform is grassroots [51,54] if it can have
multiple instances that can (i ) operate independently of each other and of any
global resource other than the network, and (ii ) coalesce into ever larger in-
stances, possibly resulting in a single global instance.
GLP. Grassroots Logic Programs (GLP) [53] is a concurrent logic programming
language in which logic variables are partitioned into paired readers X? and
writers X, each occurring at most once (single-occurrence, SO), with a variable
occurring in a clause iff its paired variable also does (single-reader single-writer,
SRSW). GLP eschews unification in favour of simple term matching, is linear-
logic-like [17], and is futures/promises-like [5,16]: each assignment X := T is
2       E. Shapiro

produced at most once via the sole occurrence of a writer (promise) X, and
consumed at most once via the sole occurrence of its paired reader (future) X?;
the term T may contain further readers and/or writers, enabling the concise
expression of rich multidirectional communication modalities.
    Being relational and nondeterministic (and thus not functional), GLP—like
its predecessor concurrent logic languages [48]—can express nondeterministic
asynchronous concurrent processes such as the fair merging of streams. The
SRSW restriction is what makes GLP suitable for grassroots multiagent dis-
tributed execution: each variable assignment has exactly one producer and one
consumer, so an assignment can be communicated asynchronously from its writer
to its reader without consensus. The intended deployment of GLP is grassroots
systems consisting of people operating their smartphones.
From specifications to implementations. Prior work has defined concur-
rent and multiagent nondeterministic operational semantics for GLP, cGLP and
maGLP [53], via transition systems and multiagent transition systems [49,29],
and a type and module system for GLP [55]. Here, we derive from cGLP and
maGLP deterministic counterparts dGLP and madGLP, and prove each of them
correct with respect to its abstract counterpart, madGLP for authorised runs
(Section 6.7).
Implementing a shared variable, by example. A maGLP shared pair cou-
ples a writer at one agent with its reader at another—say writer X at p, reader
X? at q, so values flow p → q. madGLP realises this with two local pairs,
(Xp , Xp ?) at p and (Xq , Xq ?) at q, joined by a global link : a spawned goal at p
suspends on Xp ? and, when Xp is assigned, sends the value to q, which assigns
Xq . The maGLP writer and reader are the outer ends (Xp , Xq ?); the link bridges
the inner ends (Xp ?, Xq ). Each shared variable is realised by two local pairs, the
reader of one linked to the writer of the other.
AI development methodology. The AI-based development employs three
layers of abstraction—math, an English+code informal specification, and Dart
code. Authority flows math→spec→Dart but harmonised via back-and-forth in
which running the implementation and deriving the specification surfaced de-
fects, also at the mathematical level, prompting major redesigns of madGLP.
madGLP and its single-agent specialisation dGLP serve as the formal specifica-
tions: from dGLP, AI (Claude) developed a workstation-based GLP implementa-
tion in Dart [18]; from madGLP, it is developing a smartphone-based multiagent
one. See Section 7.
Related work. GLP belongs to the family of concurrent logic lan-
guages [48,47,60,10,39]; it can be understood as Flat Concurrent Prolog with
SRSW added, simplifying read-only unification [27], with strong connections to
mode and linearity analysis [62,61], linear logic and session types [17,9,66,23],
and futures and promises [5,44]. Our correctness framework draws on I/O
automata [37], refinement mappings [1], and atomic-transaction theory [32,20],
using disjoint substitution commutativity (a consequence of SO) where lin-
earizability uses linearization points. Grassroots [51,29] extends the CRDT [57]
tradition with compositional Byzantine tolerance via the Blocklace [2]. We

2     Transition Systems and Implementations
This section presents transition systems and implementations among them, pro-
viding the mathematical foundation for proving that one transition system cor-
rectly implements another.

2.1   Transition Systems
Definition 2.1 (Transition System, Computation, Run, Safe, Live,
Correct [49,29]). A transition system is a tuple T S = (C, c0 , T, ∼)
where C is an arbitrary set of configurations, c0 ∈ C a designated initial
configuration, T ⊆ C × C a set of transitions, each a pair c → c′ of
non-identical configurations c ̸= c′ ∈ C, and ∼ a partial equivalence relation
on T , symmetric and transitive; its domain {t ∈ T : t ∼ t} is partitioned into
countably many classes, [t] being the class of t, and a transition outside the
domain belongs to no class.

c1 , c2 , . . .; it is a run if c1 = c0 , and safe if ci → ci+1 ∈ T for every two con-

Outcomes. A run is complete if it is infinite or ends in a terminal configuration.
GLP transition systems additionally carry an outcome: a domain-specific func-
tion from complete runs to an outcome space—for GLP, the computed answer
(Section 3). Outcomes serve only to state outcome-completeness of implementa-

Liveness. A transition in no class may occur in a correct run and is never
required to [29]. The ∼ of cGLP (Section 3) and of dGLP are equivalences,
every transition in a class; those of maGLP and madGLP (Section 5) leave the
person’s input outside their domain.



2.2   Implementations


Definition 2.2 (Implementation).                  Given two transition systems
T S = (C, c0 , T, ∼) (the specification) and T S ′ = (C ′ , c′0 , T ′ , ∼′ ), an im-
plementation of T S by T S ′ is a function τ : C ′ → C where τ (c′0 ) = c0 , in
which case the pair (T S ′ , τ ) is referred to as an implementation of T S.
   Given a computation r′ = c′1 → c′2 → . . . of T S ′ , τ (r′ ) is the (possibly
empty) computation τ (c′1 ) → τ (c′2 ) → . . . obtained from the sequence by removing
consecutively repetitive elements so that τ (r′ ) has no stutter transitions of the
form c → c.

    The implementation mapping τ (written σ in [49,29]; we keep σ for substitu-
tions) need not preserve transitions: an implementation transition c′ → d′ may
map to a stutter (τ (c′ ) = τ (d′ )), or two consecutive specification transitions may
correspond to a single implementation transition. Correctness of the implemen-
tation is captured by the conditions of Definition 2.3, which depend in turn on
the outcome function defined above.

Definition 2.3 (Correct and Outcome-Complete Implementation).
The implementation (T S ′ , τ ) of T S is:
– correct if τ maps every correct run of T S ′ to a correct run of T S
– outcome-complete if for every complete T S run with outcome O, some com-
  plete T S ′ run has outcome O

   Correctness carries two obligations on the non-stutter image τ (r′ ): that it is
a run of T S—its consecutive distinct configurations are transitions of T S—and
that it is live. We require outcome-completeness rather than run-completeness
(every correct T S run being a τ -image), because the agent-deterministic im-
plementations below realise every specification outcome without matching the
specification’s reduction order.

3     GLP and Its Implementation

3.1   GLP Syntax and Operational Semantics

This section presents Grassroots Logic Programs (GLP), a concurrent logic pro-
gramming language in which logic variables are partitioned into paired readers
and writers.
Definition 3.1 (GLP Variables). Let V denote the set of writers (identifiers
beginning with uppercase). Define V? = {X? | X ∈ V}, called readers. The set
of all GLP variables is V̂ = V ∪ V?. A writer X and its reader X? form a
variable pair.
Definition 3.2 (GLP Terms and Goals). A term is a variable in V̂, a con-
stant (numbers, strings, or the empty list []), or a compound term f (T1 , . . . , Tn )
with functor f and subterms Ti . Let T denote the set of all terms. We use stan-
dard list notation: [X|Xs] for a list cell, [X1,...,Xn] for finite lists. A term is
ground if it contains no variables.
   A unit goal is a compound term or a string. A goal is a multiset of unit
goals; the empty goal is written true. A clause A :- B has head A (a unit goal)
and body B (a goal); a unit clause has empty body.
Definition 3.3 (Single-Occurrence (SO) Invariant). A term, goal, or
clause satisfies the single-occurrence (SO) invariant if every variable occurs
in it at most once.
Definition 3.4 (GLP Program).               A clause C satisfies the single-
reader/single-writer (SRSW) restriction if it satisfies SO and a
variable occurs in C iff its paired variable also occurs in C. A GLP program
is a finite sequence of clauses satisfying SRSW; clauses for the same predicate
form a procedure. The set of GLP goals Ĝ(P ) includes all goals over V̂ and
the vocabulary of P that satisfy SO.
    The SO and SRSW restrictions ensure that GLP eschews unification in favour
of simple term matching. A guard that implies its argument is ground relaxes
the single-reader half of SRSW for that reader: a ground value has no writer
left to pair with, so further occurrences of the reader in the clause consume
nothing [53]. Program 5.2 relies on this.
Definition 3.5 (Substitutions and Assignments [53]). A GLP writer
assignment is a term of the form X := T , X ∈ V, T ∈      / V, satisfying SO. Sim-
ilarly, a GLP reader assignment is a term of the form X? := T , X? ∈ V?,
T ∈/ V, satisfying SO. A writers (readers) substitution σ is the substitution
implied by a set of writer (reader) assignments that jointly satisfy SO. Given a
writers assignment X := T , its readers counterpart is X? := T , and given a
writers substitution σ, its readers counterpart σ? is the readers substitution
defined by X?σ? = Xσ. Given a reader assignment X? := T , its writers coun-
terpart is X := T , and given a readers substitution σ, its writers counterpart
σ! is the writers substitution defined by Xσ! = X?σ. The pair completion of
a readers substitution σ is σ ⋆ = σ ∪ σ!, applied to a fixed point.
6       E. Shapiro

A writers substitution applied to a term assigns only writers; readers are left
unchanged.
Definition 3.6 (GLP Renaming, Renaming Apart). A GLP renaming
is an injective substitution ρ : V̂ → V̂ such that for each X ∈ V: Xρ ∈ V and
X?ρ = (Xρ)?. Two GLP terms have a variable in common if for some writer
X ∈ V, either X or X? occurs in both. A GLP renaming σ renames T ′ apart
from T if T ′ σ and T have no variable in common.
Definition 3.7 (Writer MGU [53]). Given two GLP unit goals A and H,
a writer mgu is a writers substitution σ such that Aσ = Hσ and σ is most
general among such substitutions.
    Here “most general” is under the standard subsumption ordering on sub-
stitutions: σ ≤ τ iff there exists θ such that σθ = τ . The operational algo-
rithm for computing the writer mgu via term matching, including a table of all
writer/reader/term combinations, is given below.
    If two terms T1 and T2 that jointly satisfy SO are unifiable with an mgu σ,
then σ maps any variable in T1 to a subterm of T2 and vice versa. Hence, the
SO invariant of GLP allows eschewing unification in favour of term matching
that performs joint term-tree traversal and collects variable assignments along
the way.
Definition 3.8 (Term Matching). Given two terms T1 and T2 that jointly
satisfy SO, their term matching proceeds via the joint traversal of the term-
trees of T1 and T2 , consulting the following table at each pair of joint vertices,
where X1 , X2 denote writers, X1 ?, X2 ? denote readers, and f /n denotes a non-
variable term, a constant when n = 0 and a compound term when n > 0:
          T1 \T2       Writer X2 Reader X2 ? Term f2 /n2
          Writer X1 fail          X1 := X2 ? X1 := T2
          Reader X1 ? X2 := X1 ? fail          suspend on X1 ?
          Term f1 /n1 X2 := T1 fail            fail if f1 ̸= f2 or n1 ̸= n2
The writer mgu is the union of all writer assignments if no fail was encountered
and the suspension set is empty.

Example 3.9 (Fair Merge) The concurrent logic program fairly merges two
streams:
merge([X|Xs], Ys, [X?|Zs?]) :- merge(Ys?, Xs?, Zs).
merge(Xs, [Y|Ys], [Y?|Zs?]) :- merge(Xs?, Ys?, Zs).
merge(Xs, [], Xs?).
merge([], Ys, Ys?).
In the goal merge([1,2,3|In1?],[a,b|In2?],Out), the writer Out is the output
stream merge produces, and the readers In1?, In2? are open input-stream tails
that other processes extend by assigning their paired writers In1, In2. The first
two clauses swap inputs in their recursive calls, ensuring fairness when both
streams are available; when neither input has a list cell (both start with a reader),
the goal suspends until one is assigned. Different scheduling of assignments to
In1 and In2 may yield different output interleavings.

Example 3.10 (Writer MGU and Reduction) Consider reducing the
goal of Example 3.9 with its first merge clause. The goal and clause
have no variable in common, so no renaming apart is needed. Term
matching proceeds position by position. In position 1, [1,2,3|In1?]
matches [X|Xs], yielding X := 1 and Xs := [2,3|In1?]. In position 2,
[a,b|In2?] matches Ys, yielding Ys := [a,b|In2?]. In position 3,
Out matches [X?|Zs?], yielding Out := [X?|Zs?]. The writer mgu is
σ̂ = {X:=1, Xs:=[2,3|In1?], Ys:=[a,b|In2?], Out:=[X?|Zs?]}. Note that the
readers In1?, In2?, X?, Zs? appear in assigned terms but are not themselves
assigned—only writers are assigned by the writer mgu.

Definition 3.11 (GLP Goal/Clause Reduction). Given GLP unit goal A
and clause C, with H :- B being the result of the GLP renaming of C apart
from A, the GLP reduction of A with C succeeds with result (B, σ) if A
and H have a writer mgu σ.
    GLP clauses may include guards—tests that determine clause applicabil-
ity [53].
Definition 3.12 (Guarded Clause [53]). A guarded clause has the form
H :- G | B, where H is the head, G is a conjunction of guard predicates, and
B is the body. Guard arguments are readers paired to head writers.
    Guards have three-valued semantics: each guard predicate defines its success
condition; a guard suspends if it does not succeed but some instance of it under
a readers substitution would succeed; it fails if no such instance exists. A con-
junction succeeds if all members succeed, suspends if any member suspends and
none fail, and fails if any member fails. The GLP reduction of Definition 3.11
is augmented to succeed if the guard also succeeds, and the suspension set of
Definition 3.20 below likewise collects the readers blocking a suspended guard.
The guard and system-predicate reference appears in [53].
Definition 3.13 (Goal Identity [53]). Each unit goal in a cGLP computation
carries a unique identifier assigned when it is spawned: the goals of the initial
goal G0 receive distinct identifiers, and each goal spawned by a reduction receives
a fresh identifier. Instantiating a reader preserves the identifier of the goal in
which it occurs.
    Preserving identity under reader instantiation lets equivalent Reduce tran-
sitions operate on differently instantiated forms of the same goal, on which the
correctness results below rest.
    The following definition provides a standard concurrent (interleaving-based)
operational semantics for GLP, which we refer to as cGLP.
Definition 3.14 (cGLP Transition System [53]). Given a GLP program
P , an asynchronous resolvent over P is a pair (G, σ) where G ∈ Ĝ(P ) and
σ is a readers substitution.
    A transition system cGLP = (C, c0 , T , ∼) is a cGLP transition system
over P and initial goal G0 satisfying SO if:
1. C is the set of all asynchronous resolvents over P
8       E. Shapiro

2. c0 = (G0 , ∅)
3. T is the set of all transitions (G, σ) → (G′ , σ ′ ) satisfying either:
    (a) Reduce: there exists a unit goal A ∈ G such that C ∈ P is the first
         clause for which the GLP reduction of A with C succeeds with result
         (B, σ̂), G′ = (G\{A})∪B(σ̂?)⋆ , and σ ′ = σ ◦τ , where τ is the restriction
         of (σ̂?)⋆ to the readers of the writers of A.
    (b) Communicate: {X? := T } ∈ σ, X? ∈ G, G′ = G{X? := T }, and
         σ ′ = σ.
4. ∼, the cGLP transition equivalence, relates t1 ∼ t2 iff either both are Re-
   duce transitions reducing the same goal (by identity, Definition 3.13) with the
   same clause, or both are Communicate transitions applying the counterpart
   of the same writer assignment to the same goal (by identity)
    cGLP Reduce uses a writer mgu instead of standard unification, and chooses
the first applicable clause instead of any clause. The first is the fundamental use
of GLP readers for synchronisation. The second allows writing fair concurrent
programs. The head writers’ values are applied to the body, whose readers are
theirs; the goal writers’ values are communicated to their readers, which are
elsewhere.
    The cGLP Communicate rule realises the use of reader/writer pairs for asyn-
chronous communication: it communicates an assignment from its writer to its
paired reader.
    cGLP has two forms of nondeterminism: the choice of A ∈ G (and-
nondeterminism), and the choice of which Communicate to apply.
Example 3.15 (Reduce and Communicate) Continuing Example 3.10, the
Reduce transition produces the new goal G′ = {merge([a,b|In2?], [2,3|In1?], Zs)}:
the body of the clause with the head writers’ values applied, Ys? taking
[a,b|In2?] and Xs? taking [2,3|In1?]. The goal writer Out is assigned
[X?|Zs?], that is [1|Zs?] with X’s value applied, and its reader Out? is
elsewhere, so Out?:[1|Zs?]= is added to σ, to be communicated. The new goal
is ready for the next Reduce, which will match the second clause (swapping the
inputs back).
Definition 3.16 (Proper Run, Outcome [53]). A cGLP run r : (G0 , σ0 ) →
· · · → (Gn , σn ) is proper if for any 1 ≤ i < n, a variable that occurs in Gi+1
but not in Gi also does not occur in any Gj , j < i. If proper, the outcome of r
is (G0 :- Gn )σn⋆ .
Pair completion (Definition 3.5) is employed since σn accumulates only readers
counterparts, leaving the writers of G0 unbound.
Lemma 3.17 (Disjoint Substitution Commutativity) Let σ̂1 and σ̂2 be
writers substitutions produced by two cGLP Reduce transitions on distinct goals
A1 and A2 in the same run. Then σ̂1 and σ̂2 assign disjoint sets of variables,
and σ̂1 ◦ σ̂2 = σ̂2 ◦ σ̂1 .
Proof. By the SO invariant, each writer occurs at most once in the goal multiset
G. Since A1 ̸= A2 are distinct goals, they have disjoint writers. Each Reduce
                                     Implementing Grassroots Logic Programs            9

assigns only writers from its reduced goal (by Definition 3.7). Therefore σ̂1 and
σ̂2 have disjoint domains. Substitutions with disjoint domains commute.

Lemma 3.18 (Reduction Set Determines Outcome) Let r1 and r2 be two
proper cGLP runs from (G0 , ∅) that perform pairwise equivalent sets of Reduce
transitions—for each reduction of a goal (by identity) with a clause in r1 , there
is an equivalent reduction in r2 , and vice versa. Then r1 and r2 have the same
outcome.

Proof. Let r1 perform reductions in order Rπ1 (1) , . . . , Rπ1 (k) and r2 in order
Rπ2 (1) , . . . , Rπ2 (k) for permutations π1 , π2 , where Ri and the corresponding re-
duction in the other run are equivalent (same goal by identity, same clause).
    Since each writer mgu assigns only writers (Definition 3.5), equivalent Reduce
transitions produce the same writer mgu σ̂i regardless of which reader substi-
tutions have been applied. By Lemma 3.17, these substitutions have disjoint
domains and commute. Therefore: σ̂π1 (1) ◦ · · · ◦ σ̂π1 (k) = σ̂π2 (1) ◦ · · · ◦ σ̂π2 (k)
    The final readers substitution σn is the composition of the communicated
parts of the readers counterparts, which likewise commute.
    The final goal Gn consists of goals added by clause bodies (which depends
only on which reductions occurred, not their order) with the accumulated sub-
stitution applied (which is the same by the above).
    Communicate transitions apply reader assignments but do not affect the
outcome: they do not change σn and merely propagate existing assignments
within goals; a head writer’s value is applied to the body at the Reduce, the
same in both runs. Therefore both runs have the same σn , hence the same pair
completion σn⋆ and the same outcome (G0 :- Gn )σn⋆ .
    Two equivalent Reduce transitions may operate on different instantiations
of the same goal—with different reader substitutions applied—but produce the
same writer mgu (since the writer mgu assigns only writers and is unaffected by
reader substitutions).

Remark 3.19 (Properness) The properness condition ensures fresh variables
introduced by clause renaming are indeed fresh with respect to the entire com-
putation history. This is guaranteed by the GLP renaming-apart requirement in
Definition 3.6.

    A cGLP run is a run of the cGLP transition system; it starts at c0 and
follows only cGLP transitions, hence is safe, collapsing Definition 2.1’s run/safe-
run distinction. Correctness of a cGLP run thus reduces to liveness.
    Deterministic implementations of GLP refine reduction with explicit suspen-
sion and failure, and reactivate a suspended goal when one of its blocking readers
is assigned.
Definition 3.20 (Reduction Suspension and Failure). Given unit goal A
and clause C, with H :- B being the result of the GLP renaming of C apart
from A:
– The reduction succeeds if A and H have a writer mgu σ (Definition 3.11)

– The reduction suspends with suspension set W if no writer mgu exists,
  but there exists a set W ⊆ V? of readers occurring in A such that for some
  ground substitution θ on W , the terms Aθ and H have a writer mgu. W is
  the set of readers of A matched against a non-variable subterm of H.
– The reduction fails if it neither succeeds nor suspends
Definition 3.21 (Reactivation Set). Given a set S of pairs (G, W ), each
a suspended goal G with its suspension set W ⊆ V? (Definition 3.20), and a
readers substitution σ?, the reactivation set is:
         reactivate(S, σ?) = {G : (G, W ) ∈ S ∧ ∃X? ∈ W. X?σ? ̸= X?}
These are goals suspended on readers that have been instantiated by σ?.


3.2   Deterministic Implementation of cGLP

Motivation. dGLP serves as the formal specification for AI to implement GLP
on workstations. The discipline is: from the mathematical definition (this sec-
tion), AI derives an English+code specification, and from this specification de-
rives Dart code.
    While cGLP (Definition 3.14) is nondeterministic in goal selection and ab-
stracts away suspension and failure, dGLP provides:
1. Deterministic FIFO scheduling of active goals
2. Explicit tracking of suspended goals with their suspension sets
3. Explicit tracking of failed goals
4. Automatic reactivation of suspended goals when blocking readers are instan-
   tiated
    The key simplification compared to maGLP is that all variables are local,
so reader assignments can be applied immediately rather than through asyn-
chronous communication.

Example 3.22 (Suspension and Failure) Consider the goal merge(Xs?, Ys?, Zs)
where both input streams start with readers. Matching against the first clause
head merge([X1|Xs1], Ys1, [X1?|Zs1?]) fails at position 1: Xs? is a reader
and [X1|Xs1] is a compound term, so no writer mgu exists. However, if Xs?
were instantiated to a list cell (e.g., [1|Xs1?]), a writer mgu would exist. Hence
the reduction suspends with suspension set W = {Xs?}. The goal is suspended
until Xs? is assigned.
   Conversely, merge(hello, Ys?, Zs) fails outright against all clauses: the
constant hello cannot match any clause head’s first argument, regardless of
what readers are instantiated to.

Definition 3.23 (dGLP Configuration). A dGLP configuration over
program P is a triple (Q, S, F ) where:
– Q is a sequence (queue) of active unit goals
– S is a set of suspended unit goals, each paired with its suspension set of
  blocking readers
– F is a set of failed unit goals

Definition 3.24 (dGLP Transition System).                        The transition system
dGLP(P ) = (C, c0 , T , id) over GLP program P and initial goal G0 satisfying SO
is defined by:

Remark 3.32 (Step-by-Step vs. Outcome Equivalence) Step-by-step
completeness would require dGLP to match cGLP’s transitions in order, which
is impossible due to cGLP’s nondeterministic goal selection vs. dGLP’s FIFO
scheduling. Outcome-completeness is the appropriate notion: what matters for
program correctness is the final result (the clause derived and final substitution),
not the intermediate reduction order.
Bounded Tail Reduction. Strict FIFO appends every body goal to the back
of Q (Definition 3.24), so a tail-recursive goal—one whose body is a single
continuation—is enqueued and dequeued at every reduction. A workstation im-
plementation avoids this churn by reducing the continuation in place, ahead of
the queue; left unbounded, this lets one goal monopolise the engine and starve
the goals already queued behind it, breaking liveness. dGLP therefore admits a
bounded tail optimisation, realised in the bytecode by the requeue instruction
(Section B.3).
Definition 3.33 (b-Bounded Tail Schedule). Fix b ≥ 1. In a Reduce of the
queue head A, at most one goal of the body B is designated the tail goal A′ ;
the remaining goals of B and the reactivated goals R are appended to the back
of Q as in Definition 3.24. A b-bounded tail schedule relaxes the FIFO order
of Remark 3.25 only as follows: the tail goal A′ may be reduced ahead of the
goals standing before it in Qr , for at most b such tail reductions in succession;
after b consecutive tail reductions the next tail goal is appended to the back of Q,
restoring FIFO.
    The bound is finite but otherwise free: its value trades scheduling overhead
against the latency the queued goals see, and carries no semantic weight; the
implementation fixes it (currently b = 26). At b = 1 the schedule is strict
FIFO—each tail goal is reduced once and then yields—correct, but forgoing
the optimisation.
Corollary 3.34 (Bounded Tail Reduction Preserves Correctness) For
every b ≥ 1, (dGLP, τ ) confined to b-bounded tail schedules is a correct and
outcome-complete implementation of cGLP.
Proof. A b-bounded tail schedule performs exactly the Reduce transitions of the
corresponding strict-FIFO run, only in a different order: each tail goal is reduced
earlier than its FIFO position, and every goal standing behind it is delayed by at
most b further reductions. Outcome-completeness is therefore immediate from
Lemma 3.18, which depends on the set of reductions performed and not on
their order. For correctness (liveness), b is finite, so the in-place continuation
always yields within b steps; hence every goal that enters Q reaches the head in
finitely many transitions and every enabled Reduce class is eventually taken, as
in Lemma 3.28.

5     Multiagent GLP and Its Implementation
5.1    Multiagent GLP
This section recalls maGLP—the multiagent operational semantics of
GLP [53]—a transactions-based multiagent transition system (Section 4).
From GLP to Multiagent GLP. In extending GLP to multiple agents, each
agent maintains its own asynchronous resolvent as its local state. GLP’s vari-
able pairs provide natural asynchronous channels for inter-agent communication:
when agent p assigns a writer X for which the paired reader X? is held by agent
q, the assignment X := T must be communicated to q.
    A key difference between cGLP and maGLP is in the initial state. In a
multiagent transition system all agents must have the same initial local state s0
(Definition 4.3). This precludes setting up an initial configuration in which agents
share logic variables, as this would imply different initial states for different
agents.
    We resolve this in two steps. First, we employ only anonymous logic variables
“_” in the initial local states of agents: Anonymous variables are, on the one hand,
syntactically identical, hence allow all initial states to be syntactically identical,
and on the other hand represent unique variables, hence semantically all initial
goals have unique, local, non-shared variables. The initial state of all agents is the
atomic goal agent(ch(_?,_),ch(_?,_)), providing two bidirectional channels:
the first to the person operating the machine, and the second to the network.
The runtime writes its two anonymous readers: the person’s responses to the
first (the Input transaction below), and other agents’ messages to the second
(the Cold-call transaction below); a clause has no anonymous reader [53]. The
first channel is a bidirectional stream of GLP messages between the machine
and the person, mediated by runtime UI support below the GLP layer and by
physical UI hardware; messages flowing toward the person are rendered as UI
16      E. Shapiro

elements, and the person’s responses flow back as GLP messages. With appropri-
ate UI runtime support, a GLP message to the person may contain a writer, in
which case the runtime presents it as a question and an eventual response from
the person becomes the corresponding assignment. The second channel carries
communication with other agents.
    Second, the Cold-call transaction enables agents to bootstrap communication
by establishing shared variables through the network infrastructure, realising the
cold-call protocol for connecting previously-disconnected agents.

5.2   Implementing Multiagent GLP with Deterministic Agents
Motivation. madGLP serves as the formal specification for AI to implement
maGLP on smartphones communicating peer-to-peer, without reliance on cen-
tral servers. The discipline is: from the mathematical definition, AI derives an
English+code specification, and from this specification derives Dart code.
    This section specifies madGLP, an agent-deterministic transition system that
implements maGLP by replacing each cross-agent shared variable pair with two
local pairs—the reader of one linked to the writer of the other by a global link,
realised through a global writers table and message passing, with forwarding
handled by spawned GLP goals. A value crosses a link once, to the reader’s
current holder, and the holder’s acknowledgement releases the link.
Realising the UI Channel. The maGLP initial state provides two bidirec-
tional channels (Section 5.1): one to the person operating the machine, one to
the network. madGLP implements the second via the global-link machinery de-
scribed below. The first is realised by the Dart runtime below the madGLP layer,
which bridges GLP messages and the physical UI hardware: outgoing GLP mes-
sages on this channel are rendered as UI elements, and the person’s responses,
captured by event handlers, flow back as GLP messages. When a message to the
person contains a writer, the runtime presents it as a prompt and treats the per-
son’s response as the corresponding assignment, performed on the agent’s local
state: the Input transaction (Definition 6.21), which in madGLP as in maGLP
is the person’s and belongs to no class.
Implementing Shared Variables via Local Pairs. A maGLP shared vari-
able pair (X, X?) with writer X at agent p and reader X? at agent q can be
implemented by two local pairs, the reader of p’s pair linked to the writer of q’s
pair by a global link :
– At agent p: a local pair (Xp , Xp ?) where both variables remain in p’s resolvent
– At agent q: a local pair (Xq , Xq ?) where both variables remain in q’s resolvent
– A global link connecting the reader Xp ? to the writer Xq , realised as a
   global_send goal at p that monitors Xp ? and an entry in q’s global writ-
   ers table that maps the global name to Xq

Xp ?. A spawned global_send goal detects this and sends the assignment mes-
sage to q, where T is localized (Definition 5.10). Upon receipt, q looks up the
target writer in its global writers table, assigns Xq := Tq↓ , and removes the entry.
This makes the value available through Xq ? in q’s resolvent. Here T ↑ and Tq↓
denote the globalized and localized forms of a term—local variables replaced by
global names, and conversely. Beside its global writers table, each agent p keeps
a set Up of imported-writer records: a record (Y, o, k, s) marks the local writer Y
as the sending end of the link anchored by _w(o, k), with s the agent the name
came from.

the value carried; a request for a pending value redirects it to the requester.
Writer-name and serializer values are not acknowledged—their entries are fixed.
Traffic matching no entry or pending value is stale (indices are never reused)
and is dropped, unless it reaches an end its receiver lost in a crash, which the
receiver answers (Section 6.5).
Held Links. A link end whose other end is at an agent this runtime did not
exchange the name with is held : nothing is sent on it, no assignment arriving on it
is applied, and the runtime reports the link on the agent’s network input stream.
Four cases arise. At the holder of a forwarded reader, the request it creates is held,
and at the holder of a forwarded writer, the value its global_send goal produces
is held. At the anchor, an arriving request is held, and so is an assignment arriving
from an agent the anchor did not export the name to. The system predicate
authorise_link/2 answers: authorise, and the runtime releases what it held;
refuse, and it drops the link. How the holder’s agent comes to know the anchor
is decided above madGLP. A forwarded link is held at both ends, so two people
                                    Implementing Grassroots Logic Programs         21

are asked: the holder before anything is sent on the link, and the anchor when
the forwarded traffic reaches it. Section 6.7 gives the definitions.
   Global links parallel the encodings of synchronous into asynchronous commu-
nication in the π-calculus [41,22,8]; Remark 5.12 records the design alternative
and the cost.

Remark 5.12 (Global-Link Design and Cost) The alternative we devel-
oped first and eliminated is variable migration—moving one end of a shared
pair to the other end’s agent—whose defects are recorded in Appendix D. A
global link costs one global_send goal at one end and one global writers
table entry at the other; the goal and entry are released when the value is
acknowledged. Message traffic per reader-name assignment is the value and its
acknowledgement, plus one request after a re-export; a writer-name assignment
is one message, however far the writer has been forwarded.

madGLP Local States and Transitions. Each madGLP agent executes its
local resolvent with FIFO scheduling, tracking three kinds of goals: active (ready
to reduce), suspended (waiting on readers), and failed. A reduction succeeds,
suspends on a set W of readers, or fails (Definition 3.20); when a reader in W
is later instantiated, the suspended goal is reactivated (Definition 3.21).
    madGLP defines three transactions. Reduce, unary, performs a local reduction
step at one agent. Send p to q, binary, takes a message from p’s outgoing messages
and applies it at q—a value is assigned to the local writer recorded for it in
q’s global writers table, reactivating any goals suspended on the corresponding
reader, and is acknowledged; a request directs a pending value to its sender; an
acknowledgement releases the pending value. Input, unary, writes the person’s
response at their agent, and belongs to no class. Cold-calls—messages between
agents with no prior shared variables—are realised uniformly through a reserved
index-0 serializer that lets multiple senders append to an agent’s network input
stream, so that a cold call is a Send like any other. Message delivery is the liveness
of Send: a correct run takes every Send class that stays enabled. Two madGLP
transitions are ∼-equivalent when they perform the same Reduce (same agent,
goal by identity, clause) or the same Send (same sender, receiver and message).
The formal Reduce, Send and Input transactions and this equivalence are defined
in Definition 6.19.

6     madGLP Specification

This section contains the complete madGLP specification: full definitions, re-
marks, and correctness proofs. It serves as the formal specification from which
AI derives the English+code spec and Dart implementation.


6.1   Global Variable Names

An agent counts its epochs: it starts in epoch 1, and a restart after a crash opens
the next epoch (§6.9).
Definition 6.1 (Epoch). The epoch of agent p is an integer ep ≥ 1 of its
local state, 1 at the first start of p.
Definition 6.2 (Global Variable Name). A global variable name is a
term of the form _w(p, e, i) or _r(p, e, i), where p ∈ Π is an agent identifier, e
is the epoch of p at the allocation and i ∈ N is an index allocated by p during glob-
alization. The name _w(p, e, i) denotes a writer globalized at p, and _r(p, e, i)
denotes a reader globalized at p.
    Where the epoch is not at issue we write _w(p, i) for _w(p, ep , i) and _r(p, i)
for _r(p, ep , i), as the rest of this paper does.
                                   Implementing Grassroots Logic Programs         23

    A global name has two presentations. In a message between agents it stands in
the place of a variable and is encoded as one (§B.2); it never stands for a variable
in a resolvent. A ground structure _w(P, E, I) or _r(P, E, I) that a program
puts in a message is a term, encoded as a structure and arriving as one (§B.2);
only a variable is encoded as a global name. To a program it is a ground term—
the 3-ary structures _r(P, E, I) and _w(P, E, I), the functor giving the polarity,
the first argument the anchor, the second its epoch and the third the index—
which is the form carried by global_send goals, by send_to_net (Remark 6.12),
and by the reports and answers of §6.7. Global names identify the anchor of a
global link and enable message routing; an unbound imported reader, and an
imported writer, are forwarded under their original name (Definition 5.9), so
a link’s anchor is fixed however far its end travels. Over a real network, the
agent name p in _w(p, i) and _r(p, i) is the public key of p (Remark 4.1); the
simulation realisation uses symbolic agent names.

6.2   Global Writers Table
The global writers table tracks local writers that await incoming assignments
from remote agents. Each entry maps a global name to the local writer that will
be assigned when a message arrives.
Definition 6.3 (Global Writers Table Entry). A global writers table
entry at agent p is either:
– (X, q) for entries created by Globalize: X ∈ V is a local writer that will be
  assigned when a value arrives on the entry’s name, and q ∈ Π is the agent
  the name was exported to. A value from any other agent reaches the entry
  only after a forwarding, and is held (§6.7)
– (X, q, i) for entries created by Localize: X ∈ V is a local writer, q ∈ Π is the
  remote agent, and i ∈ N is the index in q’s global name (needed to match
  incoming messages)
Definition 6.4 (Global Writers Table). The global writers table Wp of
agent p is an array of global writers table entries. For entries created by Globalize
at index i, the index i is the index in the global name _w(p, i). For entries created
by Localize, the entry stores the remote index explicitly.
Definition 6.5 (Imported-Writer Records).                The imported-writer
records Up of agent p is a set of quadruples (Y, o, k, s), where Y ∈ V is a local
writer created by Localize from the global name _w(o, k), o ∈ Π is the link’s
anchor, k ∈ N its index at o, and s ∈ Π the agent the name came from. The
record marks Y as the sending end of that link. It is removed when the value is
sent, and when Y is re-exported and the name forwarded (Definition 5.9).

Remark 6.6 (What the Global Writers Table Stores) The table con-
tains only writers that await incoming assignments. When agent p globalizes
a writer Y , it creates an entry for Y because p will receive the assignment:
the agent the name was exported to holds the writer and sends the value back,
or, after a forwarding, whichever agent then holds it. When agent q localizes
24     E. Shapiro

a reader global name _r(p, i), it creates an entry for its local writer because q
will receive the assignment. No entries are created for outgoing links—those are
handled by global_send goals; an outgoing link whose writer was imported also
carries a record (Definition 6.5).


6.3   The global_send Predicate

Outgoing communication is handled by spawned goals rather than by table
entries. This uniform approach correctly handles all cases, including forward-
ing when both ends of a variable pair are exported. The system predicate
global_send/3 takes a reader T whose value will be sent once known, a global
variable name G (either _w(p, i) or _r(p, i)) identifying the link, and a destina-
tion agent Q.
Definition 6.7 (global_send Predicate). The system predicate global_send/3
is defined as:
global_send(T, G, Q) :- known(T?) | ’_send’(T?, G?, Q?).
   The guard known(T?) succeeds when T is assigned a non-variable term; the
builtin ’_send’(T?, G?, Q?) globalizes T and adds message (G := T ↑ , Q) to
the agent’s outgoing message set. A sent reader-name value remains with its
sender, pending, until acknowledged (§6.5), so a later request can redirect it to
the reader’s current holder. The full guard and body-kernel tables, including the
row for ’_send’, are catalogued in the companion GLP paper [53].

Remark 6.8 (Forwarding via global_send) When an agent exports both
ends of a variable pair (e.g., sending [X, X?] to another agent, or X to one
agent and X? to another), the Globalize operation spawns a global_send goal
for the exported reader. If a value arrives on one global link and is assigned to
a local writer X, then X? becomes known, triggering any global_send goal
watching X?. This automatically forwards the value without requiring special
forwarding logic in the Send transaction.

6.5   Requests and Acknowledgements

Four message kinds cross agent boundaries: a value G := T ↑ , a request req(G)
and an acknowledgement ack(G), these two carrying reader names only, and
gone(G), which answers traffic for an end its receiver lost (below).
– A request is sent by an agent that localizes a forwarded reader name (Defini-
  tion 5.10): the anchor did not itself export the name to that agent, and the
  request is how the anchor learns the reader’s current holder.
– On receiving req(_r(p, i)) from requester s, the anchor p records s among the
  link’s holders; if the value is pending, p addresses it to s as well. Delivery is
  unordered, so when a reader is forwarded twice the two holders’ requests reach
  p in either order and p cannot tell the current holder from the past one: it sends
  the value to each, a past holder discards it (below), and the current holder
  acknowledges. A request repeating a (link, requester) pair already recorded
  — the link held, authorised or closed — is dropped.
– On receiving a value _r(p, i) := T ↑ , the holder assigns the entry’s writer,
  removes the entry, and acknowledges to the sender. Writer-name values are not
  acknowledged—a writer-name value always finds its fixed anchor entry—and
  neither are serializer values, the index-0 entry being permanent. A forwarded
                                     Implementing Grassroots Logic Programs           27

   writer needs no request either: _w(o, k) names its destination, so the value
   reaches the anchor’s entry from whichever agent holds the name.
– On receiving ack(_r(p, i)) from s, the anchor records s as the holder of every
   link whose own reader name occurs in the acknowledged value—s localized
   them—and re-addresses their pending values to s; it then removes the ac-
   knowledged value: the link is closed.
– A value, request, or acknowledgement matching no entry and no pending value
   is stale, and is dropped, where it names a reader the agent has applied, a link
   the agent has closed, or an epoch of the agent before its current one: a value
   reaching a holder that forwarded the reader on, a request or acknowledgement
   for a closed link, traffic for an end the agent retired. Otherwise it reaches an
   end the agent lost (§6.9), and the agent answers gone(G) to the sender, which
   retires its own end of the link.
Single occurrence gives a reader one holder at any time, so a link’s holders form
a chain and exactly one holder acknowledges its value.

6.7   Held Links
An agent exchanges a global name with the agent it sends that name to, or
receives it from. A link end at agent p is held when the agent at its other end is
not the one p exchanged the name with. While a link is held, p delivers nothing on
it and applies no assignment delivered on it. A held outgoing message remains
in Mp , marked held, and a held incoming assignment is retained keyed by its
global name. A request from a requester already recorded for the link, and an
assignment for a link already holding one, are dropped, so each party is reported
once; a request from a further requester — the reader forwarded again — is
held and reported once the program has answered the report before it, so that
authorise_link answers one report at a time, the anchor’s program asked once
for each party that comes to hold the end; a request whose link closes before its
turn is dropped unreported.
    A held link is reported to the program, and the runtime acts on it only
once the program answers. The report is pending_link(G, S), with G the link’s
                                  Implementing Grassroots Logic Programs        29

global name in its ground presentation (§6.1) and S the agent the name or the
traffic came from; it is appended to p’s network input stream through the index-
0 serializer entry (Definition 6.10), within the transaction that would otherwise
have acted. G names the anchor and S the agent p exchanged the end with, so
the program has both and the runtime retains nothing further. The report is
appended last in that transaction, after anything the transaction itself appends,
so a link is reported only once the term carrying it can be read. The report
records the module of the goal it reaches, and the record is released with the
held link.
    Four cases arise:
1. At the holder of a forwarded reader, the request of Definition 5.10 is held. A
   request is created exactly when the name was forwarded, so every request a
   holder creates is held.
2. At the holder of a forwarded writer, the value its global_send goal produces
   is held.
3. At the anchor, an arriving request is held. The agent the anchor exported the
   name to sends no request, so every request the anchor receives is held.
4. At the anchor, an assignment arriving from an agent it did not export the
   name to is held.
    The program answers a report with the system predicate authorise_link/2,
which takes the global name L of a reported link and an answer A, either
authorise or refuse.
Definition 6.15 (authorise_link Predicate).    The system predicate
authorise_link/2 is defined as:
authorise_link(L, A) :- known(A?) | ’_authorise_link’(L?, A?).
    On authorise, the builtin ’_authorise_link’ releases what was held: an
outgoing message becomes undelivered, and a retained assignment is applied.
On refuse it drops the link: the held message is removed from Mp , a retained
assignment is discarded, and the link’s entry, record, and global_send goal at p
are released. A refusal is accepted in every case, the program refusing for reasons
the runtime does not see.
    A global name is a ground term (§6.1), so a program can write one it was
never given, and a term arriving from another agent can carry one: the name
is not a secret and cannot be made one. A kernel taking a global name from
the program therefore acts only on a name the runtime reported to that goal’s
module, and does nothing otherwise. authorise_link/2 is the only such kernel
at present; any later one inherits the rule. The check matters once an agent runs
a module it adopted from another (§6.12): adopted code shares the runtime, and
without it could answer for links reported to its host.
    A forwarded link is held at both of its ends, so two people are asked and
each decides independently. The holder is asked before anything is sent on the
link. The anchor is asked only when the forwarded traffic reaches it, after the
holder has sent it. For example, Bob sends Alice a variable whose other end is at
Carol, and Alice’s agent did not exchange its name with Carol. Alice’s runtime
holds the variable: it sends nothing on it and applies nothing arriving on it, and
30      E. Shapiro

reports it to Alice’s program. If Alice’s program authorises it, its message goes to
Carol; Carol’s runtime then holds her end too and reports it to Carol’s program,
which also authorises or refuses it. How Alice’s agent comes to know Carol is
decided above madGLP.

Remark 6.16 (Authorised Runs) A refusal drops a link, so the value it
would have carried never arrives and the maGLP Communicate it implements
is never taken. The correctness results below concern authorised runs—runs
in which every reported link is authorised.

6.8   madGLP Local States and Transitions
Each madGLP agent executes its local resolvent with FIFO scheduling, tracking
three kinds of goals: active (ready to reduce), suspended (waiting on readers),
and failed. A reduction succeeds, suspends on a set W of readers, or fails (Def-
inition 3.20); when a reader in W is later instantiated, the suspended goal is
reactivated (Definition 3.21).
Definition 6.17 (madGLP Local State). The local state of agent p ∈ Π is
a tuple sp = (Rp , Wp , Up , Mp ) where:
1. Rp = (Ap , Sp , Fp ) is a deterministic resolvent: Ap is a queue of active
   goals, Sp contains suspended goals paired with blocking readers, and Fp con-
   tains failed goals
2. Wp is a global writers table
3. Up is a set of imported-writer records (Definition 6.5)
4. Mp is a set of outgoing messages; a delivered reader-name value remains
   in Mp , marked pending, until acknowledged, and a held message remains
   marked held until authorised (§6.7)
5. ep is the epoch of p (Definition 6.1), and Ep is the known epochs of p: a
   partial function from agents to epochs
    An agent is in one epoch at a time. A global name carries the epoch its anchor
was in when it allocated the name, so the name’s epoch is fixed. A message carries
the epoch its sender is in when it sends it; when q applies a message from p,
Eq (p) becomes that epoch, which is never below the epoch Eq (p) held before.
Each link end at q records an epoch of the agent at the other end of the link:
for an end whose other end is at the anchor, the epoch in the name; for an end
at the anchor, the epoch carried by the first request, acknowledgement or value
from the holder, and no epoch until one arrives. So a holder that crashes before
it has sent on a link is not learnt of by the anchor from its epoch, but from the
gone its next message on the link draws (§6.5).
Definition 6.18 (Retire). Agent q retires its end of a link by removing from
Wq the entry of the link, or from Uq its record and from Rq the global_send
goal of the link, and from Mq every message of the link.
    A retired end leaves its local variable pair in the resolvent: the writer of a
removed entry is never assigned, and the reader of a removed goal is held by no
link.

Remark 6.23 (Message Delivery) Send p to q is the delivery of m: a Send
class that stays enabled is taken in a correct run, as every class is, so every
message is delivered. The binary Send is to madGLP what Communicate and
Cold-call are to maGLP; the index-0 serializer lets several senders append to
one agent’s network input stream. The network below may carry a message more
than once, the layer retransmitting after a lost acknowledgement. A repeat is
harmless everywhere but at the serializer: a value finds its entry removed, a
request or acknowledgement for a closed link is dropped (§6.5), a request repeating
a recorded requester or an assignment for a link already holding one is dropped
(§6.7); the serializer appends a cold call once, by its serial (Definition 6.10).


6.9   Termination and Restart

The platform suspends an agent’s application, as a phone does when the applica-
tion goes to the background, and may then terminate it; a suspended application
                                   Implementing Grassroots Logic Programs         33

may also resume. The networking layer’s queue of undelivered messages survives
a termination, and the layer delivers it when the application restarts (the net-
working companion paper, Networking Assumptions).
Definition 6.24 (Suspension, Termination, Crash). An application is
suspended from the moment the platform announces that it may terminate
it until it resumes or terminates; a suspended agent takes no transition. An
application terminates when it ends while suspended, and crashes when it
ends otherwise.
Definition 6.25 (Restart). The termination of agent p’s application and its
restart is not a transition of madGLP: the local state sp of p (Definition 6.17)
at restart is its local state at termination.
    A madGLP run therefore continues across a restart, and every result of this
section holds across restarts: a message the layer delivers after a restart finds
the entry its localization created, a pending value is still pending, and a Send
class that was enabled is enabled still. The runtime keeps the local state across a
termination by saving it at the suspension (Persistence, §C.4), and a suspended
agent takes no transition, so the state saved is the state at termination.
    A crash loses the state since the last suspension. The agent restarts from the
state saved at that suspension, in a new epoch, with every link end retired.
Definition 6.26 (Restart after a Crash). The local state of agent p at a
restart after a crash is its local state at its last suspension, or its initial local
state where it was never suspended, with ep incremented by one and every link
end of p retired (Definition 6.18) but the serializer entry.
    So the epochs of an agent grow, and a name of p allocated in one epoch is
allocated again in no later one.
    After a crash every link between p and its peers ends. At p, the ends
are retired at the restart, and the paired readers and writers are widowed
(widowed_reader/1 and widowed_writer/1, the companion GLP paper [53]),
so a program waiting on one learns it. At a peer q, the end is retired when q
learns the new epoch of p, from the first message it receives from p after the
restart (Definition 6.22), or when q sends on the link and p answers gone (§6.5).
What p did between the suspension and the crash is lost at p and known to the
peers that received its messages: a state loss of the platform the agent runs,
which the platform recovers from the peers as [13] specifies, above madGLP,
making new links in place of the ended ones.
    A restart may load a new version of the agent’s program, as when the appli-
cation is updated. The local state is then restored under the new version when
every procedure called by a goal of the resolvent is defined in the new version
with the same type identity [55]; otherwise the update is refused, and the agent
restarts under the version it terminated under, which is the version the layer
then attests. A run across an update is a run under the earlier version up to
the restart and under the new version after it. A developer keeps an update
compatible by keeping those procedures and their types.
    The person’s screen is not part of the local state. At a restart the runtime
grants refresh, an Input addressed to no construct, in the person’s place, where
34     E. Shapiro

it holds the person writer of a person channel of the root’s Input type; on it the
dispatcher has every construct draw again what it last drew [56], so the screen
is drawn from the restored state.

Remark 6.27 (Globalize-Localize Correspondence) The pairing between
Globalize and Localize ensures correct dataflow:
– Writer globalized at p: Globalize creates entry (Y, q) at p. Localize records
  (Yq , p, i, p), spawns global_send(Yq ?, _w(p, i), p) at q, and puts Yq (writer)
  in q’s term. When q assigns Yq , the spawned goal fires and sends the value to
  p, where the entry routes it to Y , making it available via Y ?.
– Reader globalized at p: Globalize spawns global_send(Y ?, _r(p, i), q) at
  p. Localize adds entry (Zq , p, i) at q and puts Zq ? (reader) in q’s term. When
  p assigns Y , the spawned goal fires and sends the value to q, where the entry
  routes it to Zq , making it available via Zq ?.


6.10   madGLP Remarks

Remark 6.28 (Outgoing Messages via global_send) The Reduce trans-
action does not directly generate outgoing messages. Instead, when a writer X is
assigned, X? becomes known, which may trigger a global_send goal watching
X?. That goal’s reduction (via the ’_send’ builtin) adds the message to Mp .
This uniform approach handles both direct sends and forwarding.

Remark 6.29 (Automatic Forwarding) Send p to q assigns the local writer
at q, removes the entry, and acknowledges. If the assigned writer’s reader (X?)
is being watched by a global_send goal (because it was also exported), that goal
will fire on a subsequent Reduce, automatically forwarding the value. No special
forwarding logic is needed in Send.

Remark 6.30 (Transaction Degrees) Reduce and Input are unary: Reduce
operates on a single agent’s resolvent, and Input writes the person’s term at their
agent. Send is binary: it takes a message from the sender’s outgoing messages
and applies it at the receiver, as maGLP’s Communicate and Cold-call move an
assignment or a message between their two participants. A cold call is a Send to
the receiver’s index-0 serializer.

Remark 6.31 (Determinism) madGLP is deterministic at the agent level:
given a local state, exactly one Reduce transition is enabled (FIFO goal selec-
tion, first-clause matching). However, madGLP is nondeterministic at the sys-
tem level due to communication asynchrony: the order in which messages arrive
at an agent depends on network timing, and different arrival orders may lead to
different computations.

Remark 6.32 (Correspondence to maGLP) The maGLP transactions are
implemented in madGLP as follows:
                                  Implementing Grassroots Logic Programs       35

– Communicate: The maGLP binary Communicate transaction, which atom-
  ically transfers an assignment from one agent’s writer to another agent’s
  reader, is implemented by the sequence: Reduce (assigns writer, triggering
  global_send) → Send p to q (applies the assignment at q).
– Cold-call: The maGLP binary Cold-call transaction is implemented using
  the index-0 serializer: the sender spawns global_send(T, _w(q, 0), q), which
  fires when T is known, followed by Send p to q (the serializer appends to q’s
  network input stream).
– Input: The maGLP Input transaction is implemented by madGLP’s Input,
  which applies the assignment at once, as Reduce applies σ̂?.
The correctness of this implementation is proved below.

Remark 6.33 (Global Writers Table Lifecycle) An entry is added to the
global writers table when a global link is established with the agent as the re-
ceiver: either when globalizing a writer (expecting an incoming assignment from
the remote agent that received the writer) or when localizing a reader global name
(expecting an incoming assignment from the globalizer). The entry is removed
when the assignment arrives and the writer is assigned. The table tracks only
those writers awaiting incoming assignments from remote agents. An imported-
writer record has the mirror lifecycle: added when a writer global name is local-
ized, removed when the value is sent, or when the writer is re-exported and its
name forwarded.

Remark 6.34 (Message Routing) Messages use global names to enable rout-
ing:
– Message _w(p, i) := T is sent to p (the original globalizer who exported a
  writer and created the entry); p has an entry at index i in its global writers
  table
– Message _r(p, i) := T is sent to whoever localized _r(p, i); that agent searches
  for an entry with remote agent p and remote index i


6.11   Canonical Encoding

The Send transaction delivers a message from one agent to another (Defini-
tion 6.22); over a network, it travels as a byte string. Signatures and hashes
of terms and artefacts are computed over these bytes independently at differ-
ent agents and must agree byte for byte; the encoding is therefore part of the
specification. What is signed and hashed is given in §B.4 and §B.7.
Definition 6.35 (Canonical Encoding). A canonical encoding is an in-
jective function e from globalized terms and assignment messages to byte strings.
   Injectivity makes every message decodable, as localization requires. The
canonical encoding has the following properties:
1. Hardware-independent: integer widths and byte order are pinned; a term
   yields the same byte string on every machine.
36      E. Shapiro

2. Address-free: in a globalized term every variable has been replaced by a global
   name (Definition 5.9), so a variable appears in the encoding as its global
   name—agent and index—with its reader/writer polarity; no heap address
   appears.
3. Globalizer’s names: the encoding is of the globalized term exactly per Def-
   inition 5.9—names are the allocating agent’s: a forwarded reader keeps its
   original anchor’s name.
4. Canonical on ground terms: globalization is the identity on ground terms, so
   a ground term T has one encoding e(T ), identical at every agent—the byte
   string over which terms are signed and hashed.
The byte-level layout of e—primitive encodings, term and message grammar,
the instruction encoding, the artefact, and the loader—is given in Appendix B.


6.12    Compiled Programs as Shippable Values

madGLP ships code the way it ships terms: a compiled program is a value of
the primitive type Module. A GLP program is a hierarchy of modules [55]; the
sender links it into a single flat program and compiles it to an artefact, carried by
a Module constant. One canonical encoding (Definition 6.35) thus serves terms
and code. The compiled program ships inside an assignment message like any
other value, and the receiving agent loads it at runtime; a goal is posted to a
loaded module by run/3, whose type discipline is the module system’s [55]. The
correctness theorems of this paper fix the program; runtime loading sits outside
them.
    The engine follows the sequential abstract machine of Flat Concurrent Pro-
log [24]: a program is compiled to a string of instructions that the engine inter-
prets directly. GLP departs only where the languages differ—the instruction set
is GLP’s (it matches rather than unifies and pairs each reader with a writer),
and the byte order is fixed to one canonical order rather than the running ma-
chine’s, so an artefact’s bytes are read the same way on every machine and are
what its compiled identity hashes. A recipient whose machine uses a different
order inverts the bytes locally before executing and inverts them again before
shipping the program onward, leaving the shipped, hashed bytes unchanged (Ap-
pendix B). A program is shipped on adoption (§B.7) and named by the identities
of §B.4; its receiver relies on the certificate’s key, one it accepts or does not [53].

table, localizes, applies the assignment, and acknowledges, closing the link. If the
reader was forwarded, the holder’s request precedes or redirects the send (§6.5);
a value reaching a past holder is dropped and the pending value is redirected to
the current one. By Lemma 6.36, π maps the result to the maGLP Communicate
result.

Proposition 6.44 (Cold-call Implementation) maGLP’s binary Cold-call
transaction from p to q is correctly implemented by madGLP’s Send transaction
via the index-0 serializer.

Proof. maGLP Cold-call(p, q) transfers msg(q, X) from p’s network output
stream to q’s network input stream, establishing shared variable pairs. In
madGLP: p globalizes msg(q, X) (Remark 6.12); Send p to q delivers the
serializer message, which q localizes and appends to its network input stream;
global links for the variables of X are established. By Lemma 6.36, π maps the
result to the maGLP Cold-call result.

Lemma 6.45 (π is Correct) The implementation mapping π is correct.

Proof. Each agent’s madGLP execution is deterministic at the local level (FIFO
scheduling). For any correct madGLP run r′ , every madGLP Reduce that be-
comes enabled is eventually taken. Each such Reduce projects to a maGLP Re-
duce via π. A produced value stays pending at its producer until acknowledged;
the current holder’s entry exists from its localization, and the producer learns
the holder from its own export, from the holder’s request when the name was
forwarded (the Send of the request being live), or from the acknowledgement of
the enclosing value when that value was redirected (Definition 6.22); the Send
of the value to that holder is then enabled, taken, and acknowledged—so every
maGLP Communicate class that stays enabled is eventually taken. A madGLP
Input projects to a maGLP Input, in no class in either. Since madGLP can per-
form any set of reductions that maGLP can (by Lemma 6.46), and, since r′ is
correct, every enabled equivalence class is eventually taken, π(r′ ) is a correct
maGLP run.
Outcome-Completeness.

Lemma 6.46 (madGLP Performs Equivalent Reductions) For                every
proper maGLP run r performing reductions {R1 , . . . , Rk }, there exists a
madGLP run r′ performing equivalent reductions.

Proof. By induction on spawn order. Goals in each agent’s initial goal are in
the initial queue. For any Ri reducing Ai at agent p with Ci : if Ai was spawned
by an earlier reduction Rj , by induction madGLP performs Rj , so Ai enters p’s
queue. Since Ri succeeds in r, Ai and the head of Ci have a writer mgu. Since
the writer mgu assigns only writers (Definition 3.5), it is invariant under any
reader substitutions applied to Ai in the intervening run. When Ai reaches the
queue head, the reduction succeeds.

Lemma 6.47 (madGLP Outcome-Completeness) For              every   proper
maGLP run with outcome O, there exists a madGLP run with outcome O.

Proof. Let r be a proper maGLP run with outcome O, performing reductions
{R1 , . . . , Rk }.
   By Lemma 6.46, some madGLP run r′ performs equivalent reductions.
   By Lemma 3.17 and Lemma 3.18, since r and r′ perform equivalent reductions
(with disjoint substitutions that commute), they have the same outcome O.
Correctness. Global links implement maGLP shared variable pairs via message
passing; Definition 5.11 treats the case of a writer at p and reader at q (Sec-
tion 5.2), with the mirror case alongside. The implementation mapping π from
madGLP configurations to maGLP configurations reconstructs the resolvent and
readers substitution while forgetting implementation details (goal classification,
global writers tables, message queues, global_send goals).
    Correctness rests on disjoint substitution commutativity, a consequence of the
single-occurrence invariant: reductions at distinct goals assign disjoint variables
and therefore commute, so the agent-deterministic, message-passing execution
reaches the same outcomes as maGLP’s nondeterministic shared-variable seman-
tics.

correct and outcome-complete.

Proof. Correct: By Lemma 6.45. Outcome-complete: By Lemma 6.47.

Proof. Follows from Theorem 6.48 together with Lemma 6.36 and Lemma 6.37.


7   AI Development Methodology

This paper offers a precise interface between the human designer and the AI
programmer: formal operational semantics serve as a specification language, con-
straining the space of legal implementations. The emerging discipline [15,42,7]
we advocate and employ is for the human designer and AI to jointly develop and
agree upon (i ) formal semantics (this paper), (ii ) an informal (English+code)
specification derived by AI from the math, and only then let AI attempt to write
(iii ) Dart code that complies with both.
     For example, the spec defines outgoing communication between madGLP
agents through a system predicate:
global_send(T, G, Q) :- known(T?) | ’_send’(T?, G?, Q?).
42     E. Shapiro

The guard known(T?) succeeds once T is assigned; the builtin ’_send’ globalizes
T and queues the message (G := T ↑ , Q) for delivery to Q.
    The AI used throughout is Claude (Anthropic). While authority flows
math→spec→Dart, the three layers were harmonised via extensive back-and-
forth: running the Dart implementation and deriving the specification surfaced
defects, several of which proved to be at the mathematical level rather than
the code level, and human review caught a conceptual error the formalism
had absorbed from an earlier draft. The remarks in this paper record design
rationale developed during spec derivation. The English+code specifications
served the derivation; their content is now absorbed into this paper’s appen-
dices.Appendix D records the substantive revisions and the diagnostics that

8     madGLP is Grassroots

A protocol is grassroots [29] if any two disjoint groups of agents can each
run it independently—every interleaving of their independent correct runs is a
correct run of the combined group (obliviousness)—yet two groups that have
not interacted can always do so (interactivity). The maGLP protocol over a
program that names no agent and uses cold calls is grassroots [53]: an agent is a
constant, a program names no agent if none occurs in it, and it uses cold calls if,
from every reachable local state, the person’s naming an agent q is followed by
reductions after which the program cold-calls q. By the madGLP protocol over
a program we mean the family with one madGLP transition system per finite
set of agents P (Definition 6.19), on the local states in which no agent outside
P occurs, and likewise for maGLP.

Theorem 8.1 (madGLP is Grassroots) The madGLP protocol over a pro-
gram that names no agent and uses cold calls is grassroots.


8.1   Protocols and the Grassroots Property

We recall the definitions from [29], and the person’s input from [53]. A protocol
is a family of multiagent transition systems, one for each finite set of agents
P ⊂ Π, sharing an underlying set of local states with a designated initial state.
Definition 8.2 (Local-States Function [29]). A local-states function S :
2Π → 2S maps every finite set of agents P ⊂ Π to a set of local states S(P ) ⊂ S
that includes s0 and satisfies P ⊂ P ′ =⇒ S(P ) ⊂ S(P ′ ).
   Given a local-states function S, we use C(P ) := S(P )P for configurations
over P and c0 (P ) := {s0 }P for the initial configuration.
Definition 8.3 (Protocol [29]). A protocol F over a local-states function S
is a family of multiagent transition systems with exactly one transition system
F(P ) = (C(P ), c0 (P ), T (P ), ∼ (P )) for every finite P ⊂ Π.
                                      Implementing Grassroots Logic Programs             43

   A correct run of a transition system is safe and live (Definition 2.1); the runs
here are safe by construction, so correctness coincides with liveness.

Lemma 8.4 (Extension [29]) Every finite safe run of a transition system is
a prefix of a correct run of it.

    To define grassroots formally, we first define the interleaving of runs of two
disjoint groups.
Definition 8.5 (Interleaving [29]). Let P, P ′ ⊂ Π be disjoint nonempty sets
of agents, r = c0 , c1 , . . . a run of F(P ), and r′ = d0 , d1 , . . . a run of F(P ′ ). An
interleaving of r and r′ is a sequence e0 , e1 , . . . of configurations in C(P ∪ P ′ )
for which there exist non-decreasing sequences of indices (ik )k≥0 and (jk )k≥0
with i0 = j0 = 0 such that for every k ≥ 0:
1. (ek )p = (cik )p for every p ∈ P ,
2. (ek )q = (djk )q for every q ∈ P ′ ,
3. if ek+1 exists, then exactly one of: (a) ik+1 = ik +1 and jk+1 = jk (a P -step),
   or (b) ik+1 = ik and jk+1 = jk + 1 (a P ′ -step).
Moreover, if r is finite of length n then ik = n for some k, and if r is infinite
then for every m ≥ 0 there is a k with ik = m; likewise for r′ and (jk ).
     An interleaving is well-defined: since P ⊂ P ∪ P ′ , the local-states function
ensures S(P ) ⊂ S(P ∪P ′ ), and similarly for P ′ , so each ek is a valid configuration
in C(P ∪ P ′ ). Also, e0 = c0 (P ∪ P ′ ), since (e0 )p = (c0 )p = s0 for all p ∈ P and
(e0 )q = (d0 )q = s0 for all q ∈ P ′ .
Definition 8.6 (Interaction, Interactive Run [29]). Let Q ⊂ Π. For a
configuration c over Q and R ⊆ Q, write c|R for the restriction of c to R. A
transition e → e′ of F(Q) is an interaction between x ̸= y ∈ Q if e|{x} ̸=
e′ |{x} and e|{y} ̸= e′ |{y} , and e|{x} → e′ |{x} is not a transition of F({x}) or
e|{y} → e′ |{y} is not a transition of F({y}). It is an interaction between
disjoint nonempty P, P ′ ⊆ Q if it is an interaction between some x ∈ P and
some y ∈ P ′ . A run r̂ of F(Q) is interactive between P and P ′ if some
transition of r̂ is an interaction between them.
Definition 8.7 (Oblivious, Interactive, Grassroots [29]). A protocol F
is:
1. oblivious if for every disjoint nonempty P, P ′ ⊂ Π, every interleaving of a
    correct run of F(P ) and a correct run of F(P ′ ) is a correct run of F(P ∪ P ′ ).
2. interactive if for every Q ⊂ Π and disjoint nonempty P, P ′ ⊆ Q, every
    prefix of a correct run of F(Q) containing no interaction between P and P ′
    is a prefix of a correct run of F(Q) that contains one.
3. grassroots if it is oblivious and interactive.
    In an interleaving, each step changes the local states of agents in only one
group; therefore, any transaction whose active participants span both groups
yields a step that cannot occur in any interleaving. The interleaving and obliv-
iousness definitions are the machine-layer specialisation, without guards, of the
volitional grassroots framework of [29].
44     E. Shapiro

8.2   Transactions-Based Grassroots Protocols
Definition 8.8 (Transactions Over a Local-States Function [29]). Let S
be a local-states function. A set of transactions R is over S if every transaction
t ∈ R is a transition over participants Q and local states S(Q′ ) for some Q ⊆
Q′ ⊂ Π. Given such R and P ⊂ Π:
R(P ) := {t ∈ R : t has participants Q ⊆ P and is over S(Q′ ) for some Q′ ⊆ P }
    A transaction of agents of P from local states of P may reach a local state
of a larger set of agents, as when p adds to its state an agent outside P . Such a
transaction is in R(P ∪ P ′ ) and not in R(P ), so an interleaving of correct runs
of F(P ) and F(P ′ ) may owe it a liveness obligation that the run of F(P ) does
not owe [29]. It is excluded as follows.
Definition 8.9 (Contained [29]). A set of transactions R over a local-states
function S is contained if for every P ⊂ Π, every t = c → c′ in R with
participants Q ⊆ P and c ∈ S(P )Q is in R(P ).
Definition 8.10 (Transactions-Based Protocol [29]). Let S be a local-
states function and R a set of transactions over S, in which distinct transactions
have disjoint P -closures for every P ⊂ Π, with a partial equivalence ∼. The pro-
tocol F over R, S, and ∼ is defined by F(P ) := (C(P ), c0 (P ), R(P )↑P, ∼↑P )
for each P ⊂ Π, where R(P )↑P and ∼↑P are the P -closures (Definition 4.4) of
R(P ) and of ∼ restricted to R(P ).
   The person’s input (Definition 5.2, Definition 6.21) belongs to no class and
may bring into an agent’s state the name of an agent outside its group; it is
added to a transactions-based protocol as in [53].
Definition 8.11 (Transactions-Based Protocol with Input [53]). Let F
be the protocol over R, S, and ∼, and I a set of unary transactions over S, each
t ∈ I over one participant p and S(Q′ ) for some Q′ ∋ p. The protocol over R,
S, ∼ and input I is F I with F I (P ) := (C(P ), c0 (P ), R(P )↑P ∪ I(P )↑P, ∼↑P )
for each P ⊂ Π; a transition of I(P )↑P is in no class.

Proposition 8.12 (Obliviousness Condition with Input [53]) A
transactions-based protocol with input over a contained set of transactions
is oblivious provided that for every disjoint nonempty P, P ′ ⊂ Π, no class whose
transactions have participants spanning both P and P ′ is ever enabled in any
interleaving of correct runs of F I (P ) and F I (P ′ ).

Theorem 8.13 (Transactions-Based Grassroots [53]) A                 transactions-
based protocol with input that satisfies the condition of Proposition 8.12 and is
interactive is grassroots.

Proof (Proof of Theorem 8.1). Let M name no agent and use cold calls, and
F be the madGLP protocol over M : for a finite P ⊂ Π, S(P ) is the set of
madGLP local states in which no agent outside P occurs, an agent occurring
in a local state as a constant in a goal, as the anchor of a global name, in an
entry of Wp or a record of Up , or as the addressee of a message in Mp ; and
                                   Implementing Grassroots Logic Programs         45

F(P ) is the madGLP transition system over P and M (Definition 6.19) with its
configurations restricted to S(P )P . By Theorem 8.13, it suffices to show that F
is a transactions-based protocol with input over a contained set of transactions,
that it satisfies the condition of Proposition 8.12, and that it is interactive.

    Transactions-based with input: Let R be the Reduce and Send transactions of
Definition 6.19 over every finite set of agents, I its Input transactions, and ∼ its
equivalence. Distinct transactions have disjoint closures (Section 4): two over the
same participants differ on them, and a Send, which removes a message from the
sender’s Mp or marks it pending, agrees on the sender with no Reduce or Input,
which leave Mp as it is. A transaction among agents of P between configurations
of S(P )P is over S(P ), so F(P ) is (S(P )P , {s0 }P , R(P )↑P ∪ I(P )↑P, ∼↑P ), and
F is the protocol over R, S, ∼ and input I.

   Contained : Let t ∈ R have participants Q ⊆ P and source in S(P )Q . Every
agent occurring in the target of t occurs in its source or in M . Reduce p replaces
a goal by the body of a clause of M renamed apart, instantiated by a writer
mgu whose terms are subterms of the goal and the clause; a ’_send’ it reduces
globalizes a term of the goal, allocating names anchored at p and entries recording
the agent the goal addresses, and adds to Mp a message addressed to that agent.
Send p to q removes a message from Mp and applies it at q: localization creates
entries and records naming the anchors the message carries and p, and adds
requests addressed to those anchors and an acknowledgement addressed to p; a
request records p and re-addresses a pending value to p; an acknowledgement
records p. As M names no agent, the target is in S(P )Q , and t ∈ R(P ).

    Obliviousness condition: A class whose transactions have participants span-
ning both P and P ′ is one of Send transactions from some p ∈ P to some q ∈ P ′ ,
or the reverse. In an interleaving of correct runs of F(P ) and F(P ′ ), the state
of p is a state of the run of F(P ), in S(P ), so no message in Mp is addressed
to q, and Send p to q is not enabled; likewise the reverse. The condition of
Proposition 8.12 is satisfied.

    Interactivity: Let Q ⊂ Π, P, P ′ ⊆ Q be disjoint and nonempty, r̂ a prefix
of a correct run of F(Q) containing no interaction between P and P ′ , p ∈ P
and q ∈ P ′ . As M uses cold calls, r̂ extends, by an Input p writing a term in
which q occurs and by Reduce transactions of p, to a finite safe run of F(Q) at
whose last configuration p’s network output stream holds msg(q, T ); the Reduce
of send_to_net (Remark 6.12) then adds to Mp the serializer message addressed
to q, and Send p to q is enabled; extend the run by it. The Send changes the
states of p and q, and p’s step is not a transition of F({p}), its source naming
q ∈/ {p}; so it is an interaction between p and q (Definition 8.6), and hence
between P and P ′ . The result is a finite safe run of F(Q) having r̂ as a prefix
and containing an interaction between P and P ′ , and by Lemma 8.4 it is a prefix
of a correct run of F(Q). Hence F is interactive.

9    Related Work
Concurrent Constraint Programming. Concurrent constraint program-
ming [46] organises computation around a shared store accessed by concurrent
agents, accumulating constraints rather than assigning single-occurrence
variables. GLP belongs to the family of committed-choice concurrent logic
languages [47,60,10] surveyed in [48], and may be understood as Flat Concurrent
Prolog [39] with SRSW added—resolving the semantic difficulties of read-only
unification [27] so that term matching suffices.
Modes and Linearity. Ueda’s moded Flat GHC [62,63] assigns polarity to
every variable occurrence and guarantees each variable is written exactly once,
establishing “programming as wiring”; his subsequent linearity analysis [61] iden-
tifies single-reader variables for compile-time garbage collection. GLP enforces
both single-reader and single-writer universally as syntactic restrictions, en-
abling its distributed execution model. The SRSW discipline connects to linear
logic [17,64,21]: each assignment is produced and consumed at most once.
Session Types and Linear Logic. GLP’s writer/reader pairs connect to ses-
sion types via the Curry-Howard correspondence [9,65,66]: linear logic proposi-
tions interpret as session types where channels are used exactly once. Multiparty
session types [23] coordinate communication among multiple agents; a compan-
ion paper [55] develops a type system for GLP along these lines. Whereas session
types address communication-protocol safety, this paper addresses correctness
of a distributed implementation against an abstract specification. The forwarder
role of global_send parallels the forwarding processes of the π-calculus encod-
ings of synchronous into asynchronous communication [41,22,8]; a global link
differs in carrying exactly one write-once assignment from its fixed anchor to the
reader’s current holder.
Futures and Promises. A GLP reader/writer pair is operationally a fu-
ture or promise [5,16,14]: the writer creates a promise, the reader awaits
fulfilment. Pruiksma and Pfenning [44] develop linear futures with write-once
shared-memory semantics analogous to GLP’s. Unlike mainstream futures,
GLP’s may carry terms with further reader/writer pairs, enabling recursive
communication structures shared with channel-passing calculi [40]; none of
these works address distributed implementation across independent agents—the
specific contribution of madGLP’s global links.
Transition Systems and Refinement. Our correctness framework uses sim-
ulation relations between transition systems, following the I/O automata tra-
dition [34,35,36,37] and Abadi and Lamport’s refinement-mapping theory [1].
We use outcome equivalence rather than trace refinement, and forward simula-
tion directly (without history or prophecy variables): the SO invariant provides
sufficient structure. We use simulation rather than bisimulation [45] because
committed-choice clause selection means dGLP and cGLP need not be bisimilar
even though they produce the same outcomes.
Atomic Transactions. Our showing that shared variables spanning agents can
be implemented by local variables and message passing builds on the theory of
atomic transactions [32,33,6] and on linearizability [20], which defines concurrent
                                  Implementing Grassroots Logic Programs       47

correctness via linearization points. Our decomposition follows a different route:
disjoint substitution commutativity (a consequence of SO) makes the order of
the implementation’s transactions irrelevant to outcomes.
Grassroots and Monotonic Distributed Systems. The grassroots
property—that any agent subset forms a functioning subsystem—was intro-
duced for distributed platforms [51] via multiagent transition systems with
monotonicity. The definition we use follows [29]. Applications include grassroots
social networks [52], cryptocurrencies [50,28], and federated governance [19,58].
CRDTs [57,26] achieve eventual consistency via commuting updates; the
Blocklace [2] demonstrates the compositionality grassroots systems require.
Related Language Implementations. Several concurrent languages share
design goals with GLP: Erlang [3,30], Go [43], Rust [38], session-typed lan-
guages [12,31,59], capability-based actors [11], and active-object languages with
futures [25]. GLP differs in its logic programming foundation, with SRSW emerg-
ing from logical structure rather than a separate type system; none provide
formal proofs that their distributed implementations correctly implement their
abstract semantics.
AI-Assisted Formal Methods. AI-driven derivation of implementations from
formal specifications is an emerging discipline [15,42,7]. Our approach shares
the premise that formal specifications constrain AI-generated code, but employs
three layers of abstraction (math→informal spec→Dart) with bidirectional feed-
back, and at greater scale: the specifications in this paper underwent several
major revisions driven by difficulties discovered during AI implementation (Sec-
tion 7).


10    Conclusion
Summary. We have presented dGLP and madGLP—deterministic operational
semantics that implement cGLP and maGLP respectively—and proved them
correct with respect to their abstract counterparts, madGLP for authorised
runs.Disjoint substitution commutativity (from the SO invariant) enables cor-
rectness proofs that carry over directly from the single-agent to the multiagent
case. The madGLP design—local variable pairs connected by global links—
provides a clean separation between local computation and inter-agent com-
munication, supporting both the formal correctness proofs and practical imple-
mentation.

B      Code Format

This appendix is the byte-level specification of the GLP code format: the canoni-
cal encoding of globalized terms and assignment messages, the encoding of the in-
struction set, the compiled-program artefact, deterministic flattening, the loader,
and the offer and handshake messages. The engine implements the sequential ab-
stract machine of Flat Concurrent Prolog [24], whose reference implementation
is available online.1 The format and engine follow that design, departing only
where GLP differs from FCP—the instruction set below and the fixed byte or-
der of §B.1; departures are noted where they occur. Programs, exports, and the
load-time interface check are specified in the type and module system paper [55],
and the instruction semantics and the signature kernels in the companion GLP
paper [53], this appendix assigning their bytes; the source and compiled iden-
tities, the module certificate, the adoption of a module, and the conversation
handshake are specified here.


B.1     Primitive Encodings

Fixed-width multi-byte integers (i64, f64) are little-endian, the one canonical
byte order; clen is self-delimiting and read identically on every machine. A term
yields the same byte string on every machine: the bytes that are hashed and
signed.
– u8 — one byte.
– i64 — 8 bytes, two’s complement.
– f64 — 8 bytes, IEEE 754 binary64.
– clen (compact length) — an unsigned integer below 230 in 1, 2, or 4 bytes:
   values below 128 in one byte; below 16384 in two bytes, the first with high
   bits 10; otherwise four bytes, the first with high bits 11. The remaining bits,
   big-endian, carry the value. Encoders emit the shortest form; decoders reject
   longer-than-necessary forms (one value, one encoding).
– string — clen byte count, then UTF-8 bytes.
– bytes — clen byte count, then raw bytes.
– hash — 32 raw bytes: SHA-256 of the hashed content. The source identity
   h(M ) is the hash of the flattened source bytes (§B.5); the compiled identity
   is the hash of the artefact body (§B.4).
– agent — bytes; its content is the public key of the agent’s person. The current
   realisation uses Ed25519 public keys (32 bytes); the simulation realisation uses
   UTF-8 symbolic names. The encoding does not interpret the content.

term follows. The kind byte is new in code-format version 2, the sender’s epoch,
the epoch in a name and the kind gone in version 4 (§B.8).

polarity 0, agent q, epoch 0, index 0). The serial is an index the sender allocates
from its global-name counter, so the sender, which the layer authenticates, its
epoch in the message and the serial name the cold call for the whole run; the
serial is new in code-format version 3 (§B.8). The receiver treats index 0 by
serializer semantics: append unless the pair was appended before, update the
entry, do not remove it.

B.4   Program Artefact
The artefact is the byte string a compiler produces from a flat program; it travels
as a module constant (§B.2, constant tag 6). It is a body followed by a certifi-
cate. Its compiled identity is the SHA-256 of the body; its source identity
h(M ) is the hash of the flattened source (§B.5). A receiver concludes from the
certificate that the person of its key vouches that the body was compiled from
66      E. Shapiro

the source of identity h(M ), and whether to accept that key is the receiver’s [53];
a compiled program carries what the load-time interface check needs [55]. The
body’s sections, in order:
1. Header — magic GLPW (4 bytes); u8 code-format version (this document: 4);
   string instruction-set version; string program name. Neither identity is in the
   header: both are in the certificate, where they are signed.
2. Interface table — the program interface, carried as declaration source text,
   from which the loader derives the type automata: a string giving the reachable
   type definitions, in canonical print (§B.5); then a clen export count, and per
   export a string name, clen arity, and string declaration text. Carrying text
   rather than compiled automata keeps one source of truth: type identity is
   source-based throughout, and equal sources define equal automata. An export
   that is the instance of a parameterised declaration D of an ancestor scope—
   an entry template, in the sense of the Typed GLP paper [55]—carries D’s
   declaration in its declaration text, after its own and unexported, and the
   type definitions include the types D reaches; the loader takes D out before
   it builds the tables, and records beside the export table the type identity of
   D’s opaque instance at the export’s type and the type identity of that type,
   which opaque_type/3 reads.
3. Symbol table — clen count; per entry a string name, clen arity, and a
   u8 kind. Kind 0 compiled — a procedure defined in this program — is
   followed by clen byte offset into the code section and clen byte length; kind
   1 codeless — a body kernel or builtin guard with no code in this artefact,
   bound by name to the local runtime at load (§B.6) — carries nothing further.
   The proc operands of §B.3 index this table. Exported procedures are the
   compiled entries named in the interface table; the loader aliases exactly them.
4. Code section — clen byte count, then the concatenated procedure bodies
   in the instruction encoding of §B.3. There is no constant pool: constants are
   inline in instructions.
The certificate follows the body: agent, the public key of the agent for which
the program was compiled; then hash HSrc and hash HBin, the two identities;
then bytes, that person’s signature over e(ids(HSrc, HBin)), the canonical en-
coding of the 2-ary structure ids whose arguments are the source identity and
the compiled identity, both hash constants. The functor ids is fixed by this spec-
ification. The key is the agent’s, which the compiling runtime makes and supplies
to the networking layer (§C.2); the simulation realisation signs with the key its
router holds for the compiling agent. The runtime makes the key where it has no
networking layer too (glpc, the test suite). It is written at compilation, whether
or not the program is ever sent, so a compiled program states which source it
was compiled from and who is answerable for it. A certificate is refused to a
compiled program that calls an OS-privileged predicate, so a certified program
reaches the social graph and the person only through variables its host gives it.
The compiled identity is the hash of the body alone, so the certificate sits inside
the artefact without hashing itself, and decompose_module/4 reads the key and
both identities from it.

   The body is code only. Source distribution is an ordinary value exchange:
the flattened source travels as a blob in a term, and the receiver verifies it by
hashing against h(M ).


B.5    Deterministic Flattening

The flattened source of a program — the preimage of the source identity
h(M ) — is the canonical print of the linked, pruned program: the program
is discovered, type-checked, renamed, resolved, and pruned to the procedures
reachable from the root’s exported entry points and the type definitions their
declarations reference, from self.glp files outside the program’s tree among
them [55]; the result is printed canonically and hashed.
    Canonical print: UTF-8, LF line ends, no comments, tokens separated by sin-
gle spaces, one declaration or clause per line. Order: (i ) the reachable type defini-
tions, lexicographically by type name; (ii ) the exported procedure declarations,
lexicographically by name then arity; (iii ) the procedures, lexicographically by
renamed name then arity — within a procedure, clauses keep their source or-
der, which is semantic (first-applicable-clause selection). Procedure order is not
semantic and is fixed by sorting.
    The same byte string is what a grassroots contract’s participants read and
agree to, what the handshake compares by hash, and what versioning names.


B.6    Loader

Given a received artefact and the offered h(M ) (§B.7), the loader:
1. Computes SHA-256 of the body and verifies it equals the compiled identity in
    the certificate; verifies the certificate’s signature under the key the certificate
    carries; verifies the certificate’s source identity equals the offered h(M ); refuses
    unsupported code-format or instruction-set versions (§B.8).
2. Derives the type automata from the interface table’s declaration text and
    runs the load-time interface checks when linking the program against local
    callers: the callee’s exported type must accept the caller’s imported type [55].
3. Resolves proc indices through the symbol table per §B.3: a compiled en-
    try resolves to the procedure at its offset; a codeless entry binds by name
    to the local runtime’s kernel or builtin guard of that name, the instruction
    (spawn/requeue versus guard) selecting which, and an unknown name is a
    failsafe refusal (§B.8). Generates unqualified aliases for the exported proce-
    dures only.
4. Registers the program under its two identities; artefacts are cached and dedu-
    plicated by compiled identity.
Admission of conversation traffic is not the loader’s: the runtime admits appli-
cation traffic on a conversation only after its handshake succeeds, and aborts
failsafe otherwise (§B.7).

B.7   Offer and Handshake Messages
These are ground terms between two runtimes over a channel between friends,
encoded per §B.2; hash values inside terms are bytes constants of 32 bytes. Adop-
tion of a compiled program M by q from p proceeds in this order: p offers; q’s
person consents or declines; where q lacks it, p ships it and q verifies it through
the loader (§B.6); the two runtimes create the conversation’s root channel, p’s
runtime creating the pair of variable ends and shipping one to q; and the hand-
shake follows.
– offer(HSrc, tau(HSrcDef, TypeName)) — the adoption offer: the pro-
   gram’s source identity, and the root channel’s type identity — the pair of the
   source identity of the type’s defining source and the type’s name (a string).
– accept / decline — the consent outcome of the offeree’s person.
– ship(Artefact) — the compiled program as a module constant. The two
   identities and the key answerable for them are in the artefact’s own certificate
   (§B.4), so the message no longer carries them.
– handshake(HSrc, tau(HSrcDef, TypeName)) — exchanged by the two run-
   times on a conversation before any application traffic; both ends must present
   equal values, or the runtime aborts the conversation failsafe, and an aborted
   conversation is silent to the program: nothing further is delivered on its vari-
   ables. Derived variables undergo no handshake.
– Signed content — sign(T, K, S) assigns S the signed term of the ground
   term T under the key K [53]. S is agent, the signer’s public key; then bytes,
   the signature; then e(sig(HSrc, T )), the canonical encoding of the 2-ary struc-
   ture sig whose arguments are the source identity of the signing program and
   the ground term. The signature is over those encoded bytes. The functor sig
   is fixed by this specification. signature(S, R) assigns R the term signed(K,
   H, T) — the signer’s key, the source identity and the signed term — where S
   is a signed term whose signature verifies under K, and the constant unsigned
   otherwise [53]. The runtime supplies HSrc and the caller cannot choose it, so
   the verifying predicate does not run the signing program.

B.8   Format Versioning
The header carries two versions: the code-format version (u8) — this document,
version 4 — and the instruction-set version (string). A loader refuses an artefact
whose code-format version or instruction-set version it does not support; refusal
at adoption time means the offer fails before any conversation exists. New in-
structions enter by instruction-set version (reserved ranges, §B.3); changes to
the encodings of §B.1–§B.2 or the artefact layout of §B.4 are code-format ver-
sion changes: version 2 added the message kind byte, version 3 the serializer
message’s serial, version 4 the epoch in a global name, the sender’s epoch in a
message and the kind gone. Opcode and name assignments are append-only: an
                                   Implementing Grassroots Logic Programs         69

assigned opcode never changes meaning, and a kernel or guard name, once in
use, is never reassigned to a different operation; additions consume only reserved
or unused opcodes and new names, so a newer runtime runs older artefacts un-
changed. Removing an operand from an assigned opcode, or an opcode, is an
instruction-set version change after which the runtime refuses the versions before
it; the current version is glp-isa-3. Forward incompatibility is always a clean
refusal, never silent misexecution: an older runtime rejects a newer instruction-
set version at adoption, and a codeless name the runtime does not provide fails
to bind at load (§B.6). A version of a compiled program is its source identity
h(M ) and nothing else: two agents interoperate on a program exactly when their
h(M ) agree, so a new version is a new program, adopted afresh, and an upgrade
is per conversation.

C     Implementation Notes
This appendix records implementation decisions of the Dart runtime derived
from dGLP and madGLP: the heap representation of variable pairs and sus-
pensions, agent execution and boot, the networking interface of the multiagent
runtime, and the type checker’s error text. The code lives in the public reposi-
tory; this appendix states the invariants it maintains.

C.1    Heap: Variables, Dereferencing, Binding, Suspension
Variable pairs. The engine follows the FCP sequential abstract machine [24]
in its heap design. A variable pair is two heap cells, writer and reader, each a
tagged reference; while the pair is unbound the two cells point to each other, so
either end reaches its counterpart by following a pointer — no address arithmetic
relates them. A variable occurrence in a goal or term is the cell itself, a reference
and not an address — the heap is no array, a cell lives while a goal, a suspension,
a wait or a table reaches it and is reclaimed by the host’s collector when none
does; the cell’s tag gives its polarity.
Dereferencing. Dereferencing follows references until reaching a cell that is not
a reference: a value, or an unbound writer (one whose pointer leads to its paired
reader). A chain never runs writer to writer: a writer binds to a value or to a
reader, never to a writer, and dereferencing checks this SRSW invariant. Path
compression then rewrites the pointer of the starting cell when it is a writer,
and never of a reader, whose pointer names its paired writer: a writer whose
chain ends at a value is pointed at the value; a writer whose chain ends at an
unbound writer is pointed at that writer’s reader, the last reader of the chain,
so the invariant holds after compression as before. Path compression is integral
to the design, not optional.
Binding. A writer is bound at most once, to a value or to a reader. Binding
to a value converts the writer cell to a value cell; binding to a reader stores a
pointer to it, extending the dereference chain.
Suspension. A goal that suspends on a reader is registered on the paired writer’s
cell; an unbound writer carries its suspension list alongside its reader pointer.
70      E. Shapiro

Binding the writer to a value activates the registered goals. Binding the writer to
a reader routes its suspensions to wherever the reader’s dereference chain leads:
to the unbound writer at the end of the chain; and, when the chain already
ends in a value, the binding determines the writer’s value, so the suspensions
are activated at once. The invariant: a suspension is activated exactly when its
variable becomes determined, wherever along the chain that happens.
Ground mark. A term that is ground stays ground, every variable being as-
signed once. So when a ground test finds a list cell or a compound term ground,
the engine marks the term, and a later ground test of it succeeds at once. The
mark changes the result of no construct; it departs from the FCP machine [24],
which has none, so that a guard testing a long list ground costs one step after
the first test.
The global_send goal. The specification puts global_send in the resolvent
(Definition 6.7), where the suspension machinery above wakes it. The runtime
registers it on the writer’s cell as a callback instead. A callback is not woken by
that machinery: a commit may bind a writer to a reader, and the chain is resolved
to a value by rewriting cells rather than by binding the cell the callback sits on.
The runtime therefore fires the registered callbacks at the end of a commit,
where suspended goals are activated; it holds one registration per open global
link, and each fires at most once. Without that pass a value written into the tail
of a stream whose reader has already crossed a link is produced and never sent.
Imported readers. An imported reader — one whose writer is at another agent
— is the reader of an ordinary local pair created by Localize (Definition 5.10),
and its paired local writer is the one named by a global writers table entry.
That writer carries the suspension queue and receives the incoming assignment
(Definition 6.22), so dereferencing an imported reader reaches an unbound local
writer like any other. The entry is found from the arriving name, by its remote
agent and index.
Imported writers. An imported writer — one localized from a writer global
name — is an ordinary local writer carrying its imported-writer record (Defini-
tion 6.5), keyed by the writer’s cell and so reached from its cell in constant time:
the anchor and index of the link its value must reach, the agent the name came
from, and the global_send goal watching its reader. The record is what makes
re-export a forwarding: Globalize reads it from the writer’s cell and removes
both it and the goal, so the exporting agent leaves the link instead of relaying
its values (Definition 5.9).
Global names in the heap. A variable that a message names by its global
name is held in the heap as a term of its own kind, holding the polarity, the
agent, the epoch and the index, and never as a structure; only such a term
is encoded as a variable (§B.2), and every structure, _w and _r included, as a
structure. A program is given a global name as the ground structure _w(P, E, I)
or _r(P, E, I) (§6.1). Ground content, such as a signed term, carries no variable,
so a variable in it is refused, and a signed term carrying one is reported unsigned.
Programs of vGLP. The linker also links programs of vGLP [56]. A self.vglp
that carries procedures, in a directory with no self.glp, is linked as that di-
                                  Implementing Grassroots Logic Programs       71

rectory’s self.glp; where the layers are not known, at a boot’s hand-over or a
single-module load, its layer joins it as an empty module, as it joins a self.glp.
The root self.vglp carries widget declarations, and the linker refuses anything
else in it, text it cannot read included. A program emitted from a directory
carries a self.glp only at or below that directory.

C.2   Agent Execution and Boot
Clause try. Head matching against a clause traverses the arguments, accumu-
lating a tentative writers substitution and the set of blocking readers, position
by position as Definition (Term Matching) of the companion paper has it; a
mismatch at any position fails the clause, whatever it suspended on before, and
a reader is never resolved by an assignment of the same match. An empty set
selects the clause; otherwise the set is added to the goal’s suspension set, the
tentative substitution is discarded, and the next clause is tried — when no clause
selects, the goal suspends on the accumulated set if it is nonempty and fails oth-
erwise (Definition 3.24). A guard is tried member by member: a member that
suspends adds its readers to the clause’s suspension set and the next member
is tried, a member that fails fails the clause whatever stood before it, and the
clause is selected when every member holds. The tentative substitution reaches
the heap only at commit, after the guards hold; a failed or suspended clause
leaves no trace.
Event-driven execution. Each agent runs in its own isolate and is event-
driven: an event is the initial start, an incoming assignment message (Defini-
tion 6.22), the person’s input on the UI channel, or a place event from the
networking layer (§6.4). On each event the agent binds what the event delivers,
reduces until quiescent — FIFO with the b-bounded tail schedule of Defini-
tion 3.33 — and then performs its Sends (Definition 6.22); there is no clock and
no polling. An exhausted tail budget (Definition 3.33) yields to the host event
loop, so timers, I/O, and the UI stay live. Every event that may unblock a goal
is followed by such a cycle, and a suspended goal is re-enqueued solely through
the activations its binding returns, so no goal is enqueued twice. Agents do not
detect or report termination; shutdown is the embedding application’s.
Boot. A program’s boot procedure declares its agents: each body goal
agent(ch(_?,_),ch(_?,_))@p directs the runtime to spawn an isolate for
agent p, create its index-0 serializer entry (Definition 6.10), and post the goal
there, every agent’s initial goal being the same, as the companion paper’s
Definition Multiagent GLP has it. The runtime holds the writer of the first
channel’s reader for the person’s input, renders the first channel’s output to the
person, holds the writer of the second channel’s reader as the index-0 serializer
entry, and takes the second channel’s output as the agent’s network output.
When the runtime spawns an agent, before anything is compiled or sent, it
makes the agent’s key pair from a seed drawn from outside the program — the
SHA-256 of the clock, the device’s hardware identifiers and the persona’s name,
which is the agent’s name p in the boot clause, combined by exclusive or — and
keeps it across restarts (Persistence, §C.4), so the key pair is made once, at the
72     E. Shapiro

agent’s first start. The runtime supplies the key pair to the networking layer
whenever it instantiates the layer, which authenticates the agent’s messages
under it, and certifies under it the modules compiled for the agent (§B.4). A
boot clause names the person scripted in GLP at p by a second goal P @p, whose
one channel argument, written ch(_?,_), is the person’s end of p’s person
channel, its other arguments ground: the runtime runs P there in the person’s
place, its writes being the person’s input. An agent learns the keys of its peers
from its person: the ground arguments of P carry the keys the person knows,
and the person gives them to the agent as a person makes an acquaintance; a
readable name is a variable standing for a key (Remark 4.1). A boot defines no
agent/2: the program it loads answers the initial goal, in a simulation as on a
phone, and the person goal’s first act names the entry the program starts. A
boot is for a simulation; a deployed agent is spawned by its application with the
same initial goal and its key made the same way, the person being the person.
At the agent’s first start the application sends on the person channel the act
that starts its program, with the agent’s own key and the keys of the peers
the person knows, none at a first start; later peers come by acquaintance. An
address the smartphone hands the application, an invitation link the person
tapped [4], is the person’s act: the application sends on the person channel the
act that opens the link, as if the person had entered its text. Inter-agent message
routing is the runtime’s, by the destination in the message’s global name. The
boot clause is stripped on the boot path, so there the @ notation never reaches
the type checker and what remains of the file is an ordinary module. A directory
loaded as a program is not on that path and its boot clause is not stripped, so
a program directory carries none. A boot program is placed under the program
root, which is therefore its ancestor; placed outside it, the program inherits
neither the root’s declarations nor the modules the root exposes.
The scope a boot source is checked in. A boot source is loaded on top of
a program already in the engine, so it is checked in the scope the engine holds
when it is handed over: the linked program’s entry points and the boot file’s
ancestor chain of self.glp declarations, the root among them. A boot source
is not itself a program: it declares no imports and qualifies nothing, naming
the linked program’s exports and the root’s procedures, since a source carrying
a cross-module call is neither a self-contained module nor a directory with a
self.glp [55]. A call to a procedure the program does not export is refused by
the check, which names the call, and does not fail at run time. A posted goal is
checked likewise, in the root and the loaded units’ entry points, and a callee’s
clauses in the scope of their layer.

C.3   The when_idle Guard
The guard succeeds when the agent’s run queue is empty and, in madGLP, its
outbox holds no message that can be sent: a sendable message is a Communicate
still to make, a held one is not. A goal whose guard finds the machine busy
suspends on a reader the scheduler holds, as a goal on wait/1 does, and whenever
the queue empties the scheduler wakes the waiting goals one at a time, in order
                                   Implementing Grassroots Logic Programs        73

of arrival: the guard is decided when the goal is taken, so waking two at once
would let the second pass with the first’s work queued.

C.4   Networking Seam
One interface. The multiagent runtime reaches the network through one in-
terface, the one the GLP Networking API specifies [4]; what the runtime relies
on of it is stated below. A simulation realisation of the same interface backs the
tests and plays, so transport integration is a backend swap. Where no layer is
bound at all — the REPL, a test run without the simulation — the runtime
stands in for a layer that observes nothing and does nothing: peer_address
and peer_app assign none, a place declaration records the place and assigns its
stream unobservable, the stream closed by its removal or a further declaration
as ever, the closing being the runtime’s and not the layer’s, a nearby or reachable
declaration stands and its stream carries nothing, and the other seam predicates
return having done nothing.
The contract. The runtime relies on this contract: an agent’s identity is its
public key, and the runtime supplies the key pair to the layer when it instantiates
it, the layer keeping it unchanged for its lifetime; communication is point-to-
point delivery of opaque payload bytes, the sender authenticated by the layer;
delivery is fair — every message sent is eventually delivered (Remark 6.23) —
and unordered, and a message is delivered until the runtime acknowledges it
(Persistence, below); the layer refuses to send a message it cannot save, and the
runtime waits for its answer and, on a refusal, keeps the message and sends it
again with the agent’s next Sends, or when the layer reports a peer connected;
the layer reports peer discovery, connection, disconnection, and reachability; and
each proximity underlay has a cold-call trust level, open or closed, set by a trust
declaration (Definition 6.9), closed admitting no first contact from an unknown
agent over that underlay. What the layer attests of a peer and its session is the
API’s to specify, the version it attests being the one the runtime gives it, that
of the program the agent runs; the runtime records the app the layer reports
attested in its session with each peer, for peer_app. The layer also observes
a peer’s address, opens a UDP path to an address, declares a place of a given
radius, removes a declared place, sets a proximity underlay’s cold-call trust level,
takes a peer the program knows, a peer’s address and a rendezvous server and
removes them, and reports entry to and exit from a declared place together with
when it stops and resumes reporting them; these back the six seam predicates
(§6.4). The runtime gives the layer a place’s name, a constant, as its printed
form.
Payloads. A payload is one assignment message in the canonical encoding
(§B.2); a cold-call is an assignment to the index-0 serializer, distinguished by
its global name, not by a message kind. The runtime signs with the agent’s key
pair, which it holds, for the signature kernels; the signed content is §B.7.
Global-name indices. Each agent allocates global-name indices from one
counter shared by Globalize and Localize, starting at 1 — index 0 is the
serializer (Definition 6.10). A removed entry’s index is never reused, and a name
74      E. Shapiro

carries the epoch it was allocated in (Definition 6.1), so a global name denotes
one link for the whole run, across every crash.
Early messages. Delivery is unordered, so an assignment to _r(p, i) may ar-
rive before the message whose localization creates its global writers table entry.
The Send transaction applies an assignment only where the entry exists (Def-
inition 6.22); the runtime holds such an early assignment, keyed by its global
name, and applies it when localization creates the entry. A value for a reader
name whose value the agent has applied already is a repeat, not an early as-
signment, and is dropped; to tell the two apart the agent keeps the set of reader
names it has applied. The record lives for the agent’s life: the sender’s one counter
numbers every name it allocates, and the receiver applies only some of them,
so no prefix of the indices need fill and no representation of the set is bounded;
bounding the record needs the sender’s or the layer’s help, and is left open.
Link release. A reader-name value is retained by its sender, pending, until
its acknowledgement arrives (§6.5); a request re-addresses the pending value to
the reader’s current holder. Traffic for a closed link — a late value, request, or
acknowledgement — is dropped; indices are never reused, so the drop is failsafe.
Traffic for a lost end is answered gone (§6.5), and a link end whose other end is
known to be in a retired epoch is retired (Definition 6.26): its entry or its record
and goal are released as a refused link’s are (§6.7), and its messages are dropped.
Held links. A link end whose other end is at an agent the runtime did not
exchange the name with is held and reported (§6.7). The test is structural —
the anchor named in the global name, or the agent an entry was exported to,
against the agent that sent the traffic — and the runtime does not consult the
layer’s reachability or trust state for it. A held outgoing message stays in Mp
marked held; a held incoming assignment is retained keyed by its global name,
as an early assignment is. authorise_link/2 releases it, or drops the link.
Persistence. The agent’s local state survives the termination of its application
(Definition 6.25). The runtime keeps it in a store the host gives it, beside the
layer’s queue.
1. What is saved. The agent’s key pair; the compiled program the agent runs,
    with the files of its directory, which load_file/2 reads; the local state sp
    (Definition 6.17) — the resolvent with its goal identities and suspension sets,
    the global writers table with the serializer’s set of appended cold calls, the
    imported-writer records, the outgoing messages with their pending and held
    marks, the epoch and the known epochs; the global-name counter; the set of
    applied reader names, and the early and held assignments retained; and every
    standing declaration — the peers, the addresses, the rendezvous servers, the
    trust levels, the places, each with the centre it was declared about, a latitude
    and a longitude in degrees, and the nearby, reachable, public-address and
    network declarations.
2. When. The host saves the state at the suspension of the application (Defini-
    tion 6.24): on a phone, when the application goes to the background. After
    the save the agent takes no event until the application resumes. At every start
    and at every resumption the runtime sets a mark, running, in the store, and
                                   Implementing Grassroots Logic Programs        75

   the save clears it. The runtime saves at no other time but at a restart after a
   crash, below. The runtime acknowledges a delivered message to the layer in
   the cycle that applies it.
3. Restart. A restart resumes from the state the store holds. If the running mark
   is set at a start, the application crashed: the runtime increments the epoch
   and retires the link ends (Definition 6.26), and saves the state at once, so
   that a second crash before the next suspension opens a further epoch. The
   networking layer keeps no declaration across a launch: at a restart, before the
   first goal is reduced, the runtime declares to the layer again every declaration
   the saved state holds, a place about the centre saved for it.
4. Failure. If a save fails, the store keeps the state saved before it, and the
   runtime reports the failure. If the runtime cannot read or cannot restore the
   saved state, as one written under an earlier format of the store, or under a
   program the store no longer keeps, the state is lost: the runtime reports it
   and starts the agent afresh, under the key pair the store keeps.
An update. At a restart under a new version of the program the runtime com-
pares, before restoring anything, each goal of the resolvent, queued or suspended,
with the new version: the goal’s procedure, named by its path-qualified name and
arity in the linked program, must be defined there with the same type identity, a
parameterised procedure’s identity being compared at every opaque instance. A
procedure moved to another file is therefore not defined in the new version. Goals
of a guest activated by run/3 are not compared. Where every goal passes, each
is restored to the entry of its procedure in the new code, and every reference in
the state to the agent’s module stands for the new version; otherwise the agent
runs the compiled program the store holds, and load_file/2 reads the files the
store holds with it. What the layer keeps of its own across a restart, the queue,
is the networking companion paper’s.
Simulation realisation. The simulation realisation runs agents as isolates be-
hind the same interface, with a router owning the identifier–key directory, the
adjacency relation, and per-pair queues; a readable name stands for a key (Re-
mark 4.1), so a destination, in a global name or in a message, is a key or a
readable name standing for one, and the seam resolves both to the one agent; a
program runs unchanged in a simulation and over the network. The adjacency
relation is total by default, every agent a PAN peer of every other, and the har-
ness cuts and restores it, so disconnection, queuing and redelivery are tested;
harness controls partition pairs and defer delivery, exercising fair delivery and
order independence. The PAN’s cold-call trust level is enforced as the trust dec-
laration sets it: under closed, first contact from an agent not declared a peer is
not answered, and waits until the receiver declares the sender a peer, opens its
PAN trust level, or contacts the sender. The harness counts the work it hands
each agent, its start and each message delivered to it, against the agent’s reports
that it has finished each, sent after the Sends that work made. It starts the run
when every agent reports ready, and fails the boot when an agent reports that
it cannot start, faults, or its isolate ends before it is ready. It takes the agents
as quiescent when every piece of work it handed out is reported finished, and it
76      E. Shapiro

waits on these reports and on no clock. Not provided: the WAN underlay and
the rendezvous server, so peer_address/2, punch_udp/1 the effect of an address
or a rendezvous declaration, and a reachable declaration’s stream, which carries
nothing, and a public-address declaration’s, which carries none; and the local
network, so a network declaration’s stream, which carries none; and the plat-
form’s geofencing, so a place declaration and its removal; the reported underlay
is the PAN throughout. A nearby declaration on the PAN is given seen for an
adjacent agent the declaring agent has declared a peer, and for any other adja-
cent agent once the PAN trust levels of both are open, at the moment the second
opens; it is given gone when the harness cuts the adjacency. The harness sets no
trust level at spawn. The router reports each connection and disconnection to
the agents, and reports every peer attested with the app the harness names, its
developer, name and version, and every peer unattested where it names none.
The harness is told the app by its configuration, and the REPL by :app followed
by the developer, the name and the version, which holds for every boot after it;

C.5   Self-Module

The kernel predicate self_module, whose body kernel is ’_self_module’, re-
turns, as a Module constant, the compiled artefact (§B.4) of the app that calls it.
An agent ships a running app to a friend by sending this value; the friend adopts
it (§B.7) and runs it. On the wire the constant travels as its artefact bytes under
the module tag (§B.2), in an assignment message like any value (§6.12).
    Every goal points to a module. An initial goal is started on a module by
run(Goal, Type, Module), and every goal spawned from it carries a program
counter into that module. Whether an agent runs one module or a million, it
is the same code. self_module is the inverse of run: it takes no argument and
returns the module its goal points to.
    The Module constant carries the artefact — the code, and the certificate
over its source and compiled identities — so the adopter verifies the certificate,
checks its source identity against the offered h(M ), and runs the code (§B.6).
The constant kind Module is the type system’s [55]; the kernel predicate and the
module-value representation are the runtime’s.


C.6   Type Checker Error Text

A term may be of any size [55], so the checker walks a list along its spine, and its
error text is bounded by the first fault and not by the term. An ill-typed term’s
error gives its first failing path and the number of the others. An input-coverage
error ([55], Definition “Well-Typed GLP Program”, condition 2) gives, for each
argument, its first uncovered alternative and the number of the others. A path
is written out only when its error is read.

D    Development History

This appendix records how the madGLP specification and its Dart implementa-
tion co-evolved. The discipline of Section 7 is bidirectional: authority flows from
the mathematics to the specification to the code, but implementation runs and
specification derivation surface defects, and several such defects proved to be at
the mathematical level rather than the code level. Human review, in turn, caught
a conceptual error the formalism had absorbed from an earlier draft of the paper.
The episodes below are the substantive ones; each names what changed, how it
was found, and which layer was at fault.
The implementation arc. The implementation passed through five stages:
cGLP, the concurrent source language; dGLP, the single-agent deterministic im-
plementation; maGLP, the abstract multiagent specification; irmaGLP, a first
multiagent implementation organised around a binary network transaction; and
madGLP, the current implementation, in which a shared variable pair is realised
by two local pairs joined by a global link, with forwarding carried by spawned
global_send goals and a reserved index-0 serializer for cold-calls. The migration
from irmaGLP to madGLP, in early 2026, is where most of the corrections below
occurred.
Localize stored the wrong half of the variable pair. The Localize operation
stored, in the global writers table entry, the wrong half of the local variable
pair: for an incoming globalized writer _w(p, i), the entry must store the local
writer Xq (so that an arriving assignment can flow to its reader Xq ? in q’s
resolvent), but it stored the reader instead. The error was found by tracing
a complete communication scenario during specification derivation: p assigns
X := T , the message reaches q, and q cannot find the local variable to assign. The
correction touched the Localize and Receive definitions, the madGLP Reduce
transition, and the Globalize–Localize correspondence lemma. The fault was at
the specification level and was fixed there.
Globalizing at production time — a layering decision later reversed.
At one point the abstract maGLP semantics was revised to carry an outbox:
each agent’s local state became a pair (Gp , Op ), Reduce queued each reader as-
signment into the outbox at production time, and Communicate was reduced to
a passive sender (which merely removes the delivered message from its outbox)
and an active receiver. The apparent purpose was to align the abstract semantics
with the implementation, where outgoing communication is already produced at
reduction time by global_send goals; the change carried no recorded rationale
beyond this reading. The change was subsequently reversed at the abstract level:
the outbox was judged too concrete for the abstract semantics, which was re-
stored to the simpler form in which a local state is an asynchronous resolvent
(Gp , σp ). The production-time, passive-sender treatment was retained one layer
down, in madGLP, where it belongs — and it survives in the global_send
mechanism of Section 7, whose body predicate queues the outgoing message
for delivery. The episode is an instance of the layering discipline deciding what
belongs at which level, rather than a defect.
78      E. Shapiro

Eliminating variable migration. The earlier irmaGLP design tracked, per
agent, which variables had their paired counterpart on another agent, and relayed
readers leaving an agent’s scope through fresh relay pairs with forwarding goals.
An audit of the implementation against the specification during the irmaGLP-
to-madGLP migration exposed edge cases in which the routing was missed or
misordered. The design was replaced wholesale by the push-based model now in
the paper: a shared pair is realised by two local pairs joined by a global link, and
every variable remains in its originating agent’s resolvent, with no migration. The
defect surfaced in the implementation; the resolution was a redesign reflected in
both specification and code.
Unifying cold-calls with regular communication. A cold-call — a message
between agents that share no variable yet — was first handled by a dedicated
binary network transaction. That mechanism was removed once as out of scope,
then reintroduced, the separate path recurring because it duplicated machinery
that regular communication already had. It was finally unified: cold-calls now use
the same global_send mechanism as established links, addressed to a reserved
index-0 serializer, making a cold call a Send like any other and aligning the
implementation with the source-language paper. The redundancy surfaced in
the code; the unification was carried through specification and paper together.
Removing the false “callback” asymmetry. An earlier draft of the paper
described the case of exporting a reader as a “callback,” a term that then prop-
agated faithfully into the specification and the code comments. Human review
caught that the term encoded a conceptual error, not merely an infelicity: re-
placing the word alone would have left the misunderstanding intact. Exporting
a writer and exporting a reader are the same symmetric operation — an agent
holds a local pair and spawns a global_send goal to send its reader’s value out-
ward once known, with a table entry at the receiving end. “Callback” wrongly
implied that one direction was primary and the other a response. The term was
removed throughout the paper, the appendix traces, and the implementation,
and the symmetric account of global links put in its place. The fault originated
in the paper and was corrected there first.
Simplifying the global writers table. The variable table initially stored
four kinds of entry, distinguished by origin (Globalize versus Localize) and by
variable kind (writer versus reader). Specification derivation made clear that
entries are needed only for writers awaiting an incoming assignment, since all
outgoing communication is handled uniformly by global_send goals. The table
was renamed the global writers table and its entry kinds reduced from four to
two.
A cold-call polarity error, found by running the code. Running the mul-
tiagent implementation exposed a polarity error that was present in the abstract
specifications, not in the code: the cold-call transaction transferred the reader
X? to the receiving agent q, whereas q must receive the writer X. Because
the single-occurrence restriction makes the globalize-reader dataflow always run
from the exporting agent to the receiver, the reverse setup was simply wrong.
The fix was applied across the madGLP paper, the source-language paper, and
                                   Implementing Grassroots Logic Programs         79

the specification together, with the affected single-occurrence-preservation proof
reworked. The diagnostic that exposed it was a runtime trace in which a goal
failed where it should have suspended:
[MAD agent2] send: globalized term =
  msg(agent3, data(.(got(1), .(got(2), _r(agent2, 1)))))
[MAD agent3] registered global_send goal: _r(agent2, 1) -> agent2
consumer_init(agent3, [msg(agent3, data([got(1), got(2) | X2])) | X3?]) -> failed
Agent 3 received a writer where it expected a reader, so its ground guard failed
definitively instead of suspending, and the goal terminated. This single line —
a goal reaching failed where the semantics required suspension — drove the
polarity correction at the paper level. It is the clearest case of the loop the
methodology depends on: the implementation, by being run, falsified the speci-
fication.
Diagnosing the fault at the right layer. The same investigation first pro-
duced a fix at the code level — a write-back mechanism at the receiver and addi-
tional table entries — which was then itself judged wrong. Tracing the dataflow
showed that the single-occurrence restriction forces the globalize-reader flow to
run from the exporting agent to the receiver, whereas the specification had set
up the reverse; the two failing tests were caused by this specification-level defect,
not by the implementation. The provisional code-level fix was removed and the
correction made in the specification and paper instead. This is the discipline’s
central caution in practice: a defect that first presents as an implementation bug
is not patched around in the code once it is traced to the specification.
A missing reactivation hook. A related implementation bug blocked all inter-
agent communication using a response variable: the spawned global_send goal
was never registered to fire when its watched reader became known, so a cold-
call expecting a reply waited forever. This was a genuine code-level defect — the
specification was correct — found by running a concrete request-and-response
scenario. It is recorded here as the converse case: not every defect surfaced by
running the code is a paper-level fault, and distinguishing the two is the point
of keeping all three layers aligned.
A suspension bug that clarified the specification. In the core single-agent
engine, the soft-fail step that advances a goal to its next candidate clause dis-
carded the clause’s suspension set instead of merging it into the goal’s, so goals
failed where they should have suspended. Fixing the code exposed that the speci-
fication had not stated the merge invariant explicitly; the invariant, together with
the three-valued reading of guards, was then added to the runtime specification.
Here a runtime bug drove a clarification of the specification rather than a change
to the mathematics.
From relayed links to anchored request/acknowledge transfer. The relay
design kept every past holder of a reader in the loop: a holder that passed a reader
on chained a fresh pair to it, so a stream reaching a re-exported reader crossed
every past holder forever. The 2026-07 redesign fixes a link’s global name — its
anchor — for life: an unbound imported reader is forwarded under its original
name, the new holder requests the value from the anchor, a sent value stays
80      E. Shapiro

pending at the anchor until the current holder acknowledges, and stale traffic
is dropped on never-reused indices. A value now crosses once, to the current
holder; past holders drop out, and a redirected stream continues to the new
holder directly. This partly reverses the “eliminating variable migration” episode
above: the reader occurrence does move between agents, but against a fixed
anchor and under the request/acknowledge discipline, so the routing races that
sank irmaGLP’s migration do not arise. Epochs on readers were considered and
dropped: the acknowledgement alone closes a link, and a request from a holder
the anchor did not itself export to is what reports the move.
An internal trace inconsistency. Reserving index 0 for the cold-call serializer
left the worked traces and the figure still using index 0 for ordinary global links —
an inconsistency internal to the mathematical layer, introduced by the unification
above. The traces, figure, and appendix proof were shifted to start at index 1.
