      A grassroots platform is a multiagent distributed system in which multiple independent instances
      can form and operate independently of each other and of any global resource, yet may coalesce
      into ever larger instances, possibly resulting in a single global instance. Grassroots platforms aim
      to offer an egalitarian/democratic alternative to centralised/autocratic and decentralised/plutocratic
      global platforms.
          Here, we present Grassroots Logic Programs (GLP), a multiagent concurrent logic programming
      language designed for the implementation of grassroots platforms: we recall the standard operational
      semantics of logic programs; introduce the concurrent operational semantics of GLP as its restriction;
      recall multiagent atomic transactions; use them to introduce a multiagent operational semantics of
      GLP; and prove multiagent GLP to be grassroots.
          These mathematical foundations are being used by AI to implement GLP as well as to program
      in GLP: a workstation-based implementation of concurrent GLP in Dart was derived from the con-
      current operational semantics of GLP; a multiagent smartphone-based implementation of GLP in
      Dart/Flutter is being developed based on the multiagent operational semantics of GLP; a moded type
      system for GLP was designed to facilitate collaborative human-AI development of GLP programs;
      GLP implementations of grassroots platforms for the social graph, social networks, currencies and
      bonds have been derived by AI from mathematical specifications.
          While concurrent logic programming and its powerful programming techniques have been known
      for four decades, adoption has been hampered by their inaccessibility to the average programmer.
      With AI as a hyper-programmer, this limitation is removed and the abstract nature and expressive
      power of concurrent logic programming can be put into effective use.
Keywords: concurrent logic programming, grassroots platforms, operational semantics, multiagent
transition systems, AI programming

1    Introduction
Grassroots. Grassroots platforms aim to offer an egalitarian and democratic alternative to centralised
and autocratic (Facebook) and decentralised and plutocratic (Bitcoin) global platforms [45]. A digital
platform is grassroots [43, 47] if it can have multiple instances that can (i) operate independently of each
other and of any global resource other than the network, and (ii) coalesce into ever larger instances, possi-
bly resulting in a single global instance. A grassroots platform aims to operate solely on the smartphones
of its participants.
     Specifications of grassroots platforms that have been provided and proven grassroots include the
grassroots social graph [44, 47] — the infrastructure platform on which all others build — grassroots
social networks [44], grassroots cryptocurrencies and bonds [42, 22, 46], and grassroots federations [58,
14]; the unifying formal framework being volitional multiagent atomic transactions [24]. Working GLP
implementations of the social graph (this paper), child-safe social networks, grassroots cryptocurrencies
and bonds [46], and secure recovery from major faults [8] have been derived by AI from these speci-
fications. The Scuttlebutt protocol and social network [19] is perhaps the sole example of a deployed
grassroots platform to date.

Grassroots Logic Programs. Here, we present Grassroots Logic Programs (GLP), a multiagent, con-
current, logic programming language, designed for the implementation of grassroots platforms. Syntac-
tically, GLP extends Logic Programs (LP) [27] with:
1. Readers: Each logic variable X (now referred to as writer) is paired with a reader X?, which is
   assigned a value only once its paired writer X is assigned that value;
2. Single-Occurrence (SO): A variable may occur at most once in a goal or a clause; and
3. Single-Reader Single-Writer (SRSW): A writer occurs in a clause iff its paired reader does.
The result eschews unification for simple term matching, and conjures both linear logic [12] and fu-
tures/promises [3, 9]: An assignment to a variable may be produced at most once, via the sole occurrence
of a writer (promise), and consumed at most once, via the sole occurrence of its paired reader (future).
    Hence, in a multiagent distributed implementation, if a writer and its paired reader are held by dif-
ferent agents, an assignment to the writer is realised as a single message from the writer-holding agent
to the reader-holding agent. The source of all the powerful concurrent logic programming techniques is
that such a message may, in turn, contain further readers and writers, enabling the concise expression of
rich multidirectional communication modalities. In the simplest case, a reader sent in a message keeps
the channel open for further messages from the sender (streaming); a writer sent in a message allows the
receiver to reply. More generally, variables in a message paired with variables held by third agents can
be used for arbitrary network reconfiguration (e.g. friend-mediated introduction, example below).
Semantics. The operational semantics of GLP is presented in two stages: First, we present the concurrent
operational semantics of GLP (cGLP), an interleaving-based concurrent (single-agent) semantics defined
as a restriction of standard LP semantics, preserving computation-as-deduction [20].
    Second, we present multiagent transition systems and their specification via multiagent atomic trans-
actions, and use them to define the multiagent operational semantics of GLP (maGLP), with (1) Commu-
nication: Named agents that operate independently while communicating to remote readers assignments
made to paired local writers; (2) Cold-calls: A means for sending a term with variables to a named agent
while retaining their paired variables locally. Cold-calls allow two agents in disconnected components of
the social graph to become the owners of paired logic variables. We illustrate maGLP via the grassroots
social graph, in which agents may initiate friendships (bidirectional communication channels) via “cold-
calls” as well as introduce mutual friends to each other. Lastly, we recall the definition of grassroots
platforms and how to prove that a platform specified via atomic transactions is grassroots [43, 47, 24],
and apply these to prove that maGLP is indeed grassroots.
Historical Context. The concurrent logic programming family emerged in the 1980s — Concurrent
Prolog [35], GHC [61], PARLOG [6]; surveyed in [39] — and was simplified by flattening (restricting
guards to primitive tests) in Flat Concurrent Prolog (FCP) [29] and Flat GHC; sequential FCP abstract
machines [16] made commercial deployment feasible. Mode systems followed, including Ueda’s moded
Flat GHC [63, 64, 62], anticipating GLP’s SO discipline as a type-system refinement. GLP can be under-
stood as FCP with the SRSW restriction added, simplifying read-only unification [21]. These languages
saw industrial deployment during the Japanese Fifth Generation project [30, 34], most prominently in the
chat product Virtual Places, developed in FCP by Ubique [60] and acquired by AOL in 1995, after which
the codebase was migrated to C. With the conclusion of the Fifth Generation project [55], concurrent
logic programming went out of fashion. Its core ideas, however, persisted: pattern-matched message-
passing among lightweight share-nothing processes, with single-assignment variables for synchronisa-
tion, found their most prominent industrial expression in Erlang [1] — perhaps the closest descendant of
concurrent logic programming — and from there to Elixir and to actor frameworks such as Akka [26] in
Scala and Orleans [28] in .Net. What was lost in this evolution was logic programming’s metaprogram-
Shapiro                                                                                                  3


ming [32, 25] and the seamless integration of computation and synchronisation through paired logical
variables; GLP recovers these, while maintaining the simplicity and efficiency of futures/promises.
AI. The foundations presented here are used by AI (Claude) in two distinct disciplines. Implementing
GLP proceeds through three layers—a mathematical specification, an informal English-and-code specifi-
cation derived from it by AI, and Dart code derived from that—with authority flowing math→spec→Dart
but harmonised by back-and-forth. The concurrent and multiagent operational semantics are first refined
into deterministic counterparts, dGLP and madGLP [48]. From dGLP AI derived a workstation-based
GLP implementation in Dart, and from madGLP a smartphone-based multiagent one in Dart/Flutter is
being developed. Running these implementations surfaced defects, several at the mathematical level, that
drove revisions to the operational semantics themselves. A moded type checker was implemented by AI
in the same way [50]. Programming in GLP proceeds through three layers of its own—mathematical
specifications, provided as guarded multiagent atomic transactions [24]; the type definitions and decla-
rations agreed for the program together with the intended behaviour of each procedure; and the GLP
code: the designer and AI first agree the types and intent, and only then does AI write, type-check, test,
and debug the code, with the human providing design-level oversight rather than code-level interven-
tion [50]. The type checker catches at compile time a characteristic class of mode errors—confusing a
reader for its paired writer—otherwise manifesting as silent run-time suspensions; a befriending clause,
for example, was rejected because a reader argument received a produced rather than a consumed value,
and AI corrected it from the diagnostic alone. A secure GLP implementation using public-key cryptog-
raphy, mutual attestations, and friend-based identity custodians is also being developed [8]. Example
GLP programs in this paper are typed.

2     GLP
We present GLP syntax and operational semantics and discuss its programming techniques.
2.1   Syntax
GLP extends LP by adding a paired reader X? to every “ordinary” logic variable X, now called a writer.
Definition 2.1 (GLP Variables). Let V denote the set of LP variables (identifiers beginning with upper-
case), henceforth called writers. Define V ? = {X? | X ∈ V }, called readers. The set of all GLP variables
is Vˆ = V ∪ V ?. A writer X and its reader X? form a variable pair.
    GLP terms, unit goals, goals, and clauses are as in LP but defined over the variables in Vˆ .
Definition 2.2 (Single-Occurrence (SO) Invariant). A term, goal, or clause satisfies the single-occurrence
(SO) invariant if every variable occurs in it at most once.
Definition 2.3 (GLP Program, Goals). A clause C satisfies the single-reader/single-writer (SRSW) re-
striction if it satisfies SO and a variable occurs in C iff its paired variable also occurs in C. A GLP
program is a finite sequence of clauses satisfying SRSW; clauses for the same predicate form a proce-
dure. The set of GLP goals Gˆ(P) includes all goals over Vˆ and the vocabulary of P that satisfy SO.
    The purpose of the SRSW restriction is to prevent multiple writer occurrences racing to assign a vari-
able; the simpler (SW) restriction is insufficient, since if a reader with multiple occurrences is assigned
a term with a writer, the result is a writer with multiple occurrences. We use set notation also when
referring to multisets.

and the goal merge([1,2,3|Xs?],[a,b|Ys?],Zs), which satisfies SO; each clause satisfies SRSW.
The first clause swaps inputs in the recursive call, ensuring fairness when both streams are available.

LP to allow writing fair concurrent programs, such as fair merge above. Note that or-nondeterminism is
not completely eliminated, as different scheduling of arrival of assignments on the two input streams of
merge may result in different orders in its output stream.
     The cGLP Communicate rule realises the use of reader/writer pairs for asynchronous communication:
it communicates an assignment from its writer to its paired reader.
Monotonicity. In LP, if a goal cannot be reduced, it will never be reduced. In cGLP, a goal that cannot
be reduced now may be reduced in the future: if A and H have an mgu that writes on a reader X? ∈ A
with no writer mgu at present, another goal with X may reduce, assigning X, and later X?, to a value
that will allow A and H to have a writer mgu. Conversely, in LP, if a goal A can be reduced now with
some clause H:-B, with a regular mgu of A and H, it may not be reducible in the future due to variables
that A shares with other goals being assigned values by reductions of other goals, preventing unification
between the instantiated A and H. In cGLP, if a goal A can be reduced now (with a writers mgu), it can
always be reduced in the future, as the SO invariant ensures that no other goal can assign any writer in A.
     Implementation-wise, if a GLP goal A cannot be reduced now, but there is a readers substitution σ
such that Aσ can be reduced, such readers are identified, the goal A suspends on these readers, and is
rescheduled for another reduction attempt once any of them is assigned.
     Despite these differences, cGLP has the same notion of logical consequence as LP.

Proposition 2.15 (Monotonicity). In any proper cGLP run, if unit goal A can reduce with clause C at

jointly satisfy SO are unifiable, their mgu maps each variable in one term to a subterm of the other. Term
matching thus performs joint term-tree traversal and collects variable assignments along the way; the
detailed definition and table appear in Appendix B.
2.3     Guards
GLP clauses may include guards—tests that determine clause applicability.
Definition 2.16 (Guarded Clause). A guarded clause has the form H :- G | B, where H is the head, G
is a conjunction of guard predicates, and B is the body. The guard separator “|” is interpreted logically
as a conjunction. The arguments of a guard are readers paired to head writers; the arguments of a defined
guard, a guard predicate defined by a unit clause (Appendix D), may also be writers of the clause.
     Guards have three-valued semantics. Each guard predicate explicitly defines its success condition. A
guard suspends if it does not succeed but some instance of it under a readers substitution would succeed.
A guard fails if no such instance exists. A guard conjunction succeeds if all members succeed; it suspends
if any member suspends and none fail; it fails if any member fails.
     Definition 2.9 of a GLP goal/clause reduction is augmented to succeed if the guard also succeeds.
Remark 2.17 (Guards and SRSW). Guard occurrences count toward SRSW satisfaction: if X? occurs in
a guard, its paired writer X must occur in the head and X? may additionally occur once in the body; if X
occurs in a defined guard, X? occurs in the clause; if X occurs in a widowed_writer guard, X? occurs
in the head, X in the guard, and neither elsewhere in the clause; and if X? occurs in a widowed_reader
guard, X occurs in the head, X? in the guard, and neither elsewhere in the clause.
Definition 2.18 (Ground Position, Ground-Guarded Writer). A guard predicate g/n has a set gr(g/n) ⊆
{1, . . . , n} of ground positions: i ∈ gr(g/n) iff the success of g(T1 , . . . , Tn ) implies that Ti is ground. A
writer X is ground-guarded in a guarded clause H :- G | B if X? occurs in Ti for some g(T1 , . . . , Tn ) ∈ G
and i ∈ gr(g/n).
      The ground positions are those the catalogue’s Ground column marks (Appendix D).

Remark 2.22 (Anonymous Variables). An anonymous variable is any variable whose name begins with
_ (e.g., _, _Out). Anonymous variables may appear anywhere a writer variable may appear; each occur-
rence denotes a fresh writer with no paired reader, the one exception to SRSW: an input a clause does
not read is dropped, and what is assigned to it is discarded. Anonymous readers (_?, _Name?) are not
permitted in a clause, in the head or the body: a reader whose writer is never written is a goal that can
never be satisfied, so a clause writes every writer it holds, closing a stream it does not extend. The initial
state of an agent (Section 3) is a goal, and the runtime writes its anonymous readers.
System predicates and body kernels. GLP system predicates such as := (arithmetic assignment), now
(clock access), =.. (term composition/decomposition), and random (seeded random-number generation)
are defined by GLP clauses whose bodies may invoke body kernel predicates—runtime-implemented
primitives not directly accessible to user programs. The system predicate’s own guards ensure that
body kernel preconditions are met before invocation. For example, arithmetic assignment is defined
recursively as follows:
Result? := N :- number(N?) | Result = N?.
Result? := X + Y :- number(X?), number(Y?) |
      ’_add’(X?, Y?, Result).
Result? := X + Y :- otherwise |
      X1 := X?, Y1 := Y?, Result := X1? + Y1?.
The base case binds a plain number; the first addition clause guards that both operands are numbers and
invokes the ’_add’ body kernel; the fallback clause recursively evaluates subexpressions. The full table
of body kernels and the guard reference appear in Appendix D; the complete system-predicate definitions
are in programs/self.glp of the repository.
2.4   GLP Programming Techniques
On the one hand, GLP has no unification, only pattern-matching and single-writer variables. On the
other hand, goal reduction may write on several variables atomically. The result is that almost the en-
tire range of concurrent logic programming techniques developed since the 1980s [39, 38] is available in
GLP, including: streams and their fair, biased, and self-balancing merge [51, 52]; channels as generalised
streams [59]; messages with reply variables and bounded buffers [57]; network reconfiguration and dis-
tributed programming [33]; object-oriented programming with mutable state via stream-recursion [53];
8                                                                                  Grassroots Logic Programs


systems programming and computation control [37, 56]; and the full range of meta-interpreters — fail-
safe, termination-detecting, tracing, and algorithmic debugging [32, 25]. Tested GLP renditions of these
and further techniques — monitors, distributors, network switches, replicators, and the metainterpreter
family — are in the programs directory of the public GLP repository.2
     Two techniques require workarounds. The use of logic variables for mutual exclusion — standard in
concurrent logic languages with atomic unification [39] — must be achieved in GLP via a monitor [15,
4], the standard distributed-computing solution. Similarly, general broadcast — distributing a value to
multiple consumers — is not directly supported by GLP, since it would require multiple occurrences of
the same reader, in violation of SO; it must be achieved by explicit distributors. We note that if moded-
type checking can determine that the values to be distributed never contain writers, then the SO restriction
on readers may safely be relaxed allowing broadcast natively; a future upgrade of the language. Excluded
is the encoding of logic programs or-parallel by the and-parallelism of Concurrent Prolog [7, 40], which
depends on general unification in a fundamental way.

3     Multiagent GLP
We extend GLP to multiple agents: Recall the notion of multiagent transition systems via multiagent
atomic transactions [41, 47]; define multiagent GLP (maGLP) as a transactions-based multiagent transi-
tion system; and establish its safety properties.
3.1    Multiagent Transition Systems
We assume a countable set of agents Π, but consider only finite subsets of it, so when we refer to a
particular set of agents P ⊂ Π we assume P to be nonempty and finite. We use ⊂ to denote the strict
subset relation and ⊆ when equality is also possible.
    We use SP to denote the set S indexed by the set P, and if c ∈ SP we use c p to denote the member of
c indexed by p ∈ P. Intuitively, think of such a c ∈ SP as an array of cells indexed by members of P with
cell values in S.
Definition 3.1 (Local States, Configuration, Transaction, Participants). Given agents Q ⊂ Π and an
arbitrary set S of local states, a configuration over Q and S is a member of C := SQ . An atomic transaction
over participants Q and states S is any pair of configurations t = c → c′ ∈ C2 such that c ̸= c′ , with
t p := c p → c′p for any p ∈ Q.
Definition 3.2 (Multiagent Transition System). Given agents P ⊂ Π and an arbitrary set S of local states
with a designated initial local state s0 ∈ S, a multiagent transition system over P and S is a transition
system T S = (C, c0 , T, ∼) with configurations C := SP , initial configuration c0 := {s0 }P , transitions
T ⊆ C2 being a set of transactions over P and S, and ∼ a partial equivalence on T .

members of Q do the same in t and in t ′ , and the rest remain in their current (arbitrary) state. The closure
likewise carries any partial equivalence on transactions to one on the induced transitions: where every
participant of every transaction in R changes its state, distinct transactions in R have disjoint closures,
and ∼↑P is a partial equivalence whenever ∼ is.
Definition 3.4 (Transactions-Based Multiagent Transition System). Given agents P ⊂ Π, local states S
with initial local state s0 ∈ S, a set of transactions R, each t ∈ R over some Q ⊆ P and S, and a partial
equivalence ∼ on R, the transactions-based multiagent transition system over P, S, R, and ∼ is the
multiagent transition system T S = (SP , {s0 }P , R↑P, ∼↑P).
3.2   From cGLP to maGLP
In extending GLP to multiple agents, each agent maintains its own asynchronous resolvent as its local
state. The key insight is that GLP’s variable pairs provide natural binary communication channels: when
agent p assigns a writer X for which the paired reader X? is held by agent q, the assignment X := T must
be communicated to q.
    A key difference between cGLP and maGLP is in the initial state. In a multiagent transition system all
agents must have the same initial local state s0 (Definition 3.2), precluding the initial sharing of logic vari-
ables, as this would imply different initial states for different agents. We resolve this in two steps. First,
we employ only anonymous logic variables “_” in the initial local states of agents: Anonymous variables
are, on the one hand, syntactically identical, hence allow all initial states to be syntactically identical,
and on the other hand represent unique variables, hence semantically all initial goals have unique, local,
non-shared variables. The initial state of all agents is the atomic goal agent(ch(_?,_),ch(_?,_)),
providing two bidirectional channels: the first to the person operating the machine, and the second to
the network. The runtime writes its two anonymous readers: the person’s responses to the first (the
Input transaction below), and other agents’ messages to the second (the Cold-call transaction below; Re-
mark 2.22). The first channel is a bidirectional stream of GLP messages between the machine and the
person, mediated by runtime UI support below the GLP layer and by physical UI hardware; messages
flowing toward the person are rendered as UI elements, and the person’s responses flow back as GLP
messages. A person scripted in GLP is a program the runtime runs at the person’s channel in the person’s
place, its writes being the Input transactions. With appropriate UI runtime support, a GLP message to the
user may contain a writer, in which case the runtime presents it as a question and an eventual response
from the person becomes the corresponding assignment. The second channel carries communication
with other agents.
    Second, the Cold-call transaction enables agents to bootstrap communication by establishing shared
variables through the network infrastructure, realising the cold-call protocol for connecting previously-
disconnected agents.

a paired variable they can use it to communicate indefinitely; moreover, an agent with two friends (with
which it shares channels) may introduce them to each other, also eschewing the need for a Cold-call.

4    The Grassroots Social Graph
The grassroots social graph is the foundation upon which all other grassroots platforms are built. Nodes
represent cryptographically self-identified agents; edges represent authenticated bidirectional channels;
connected components arise spontaneously through befriending.
    Each agent processes messages from user and network input streams and maintains an outputs list
with one typed entry per destination — user_output, net_output, and a friend_output per friend
— on which the library routers send_user, send_net, and send_friend dispatch. The program sup-
ports four protocols: (1) cold-call befriending, where agents with no prior shared variables establish
friendship by exchanging a response variable through the network; (2) friend-mediated introduction,
where a mutual friend creates a channel pair and sends each half to the respective parties, establishing
a direct connection; (3) text messaging between established friends via named output streams; and (4)
unfriending, where either friend closes the shared channel.
    The core of the program is the procedure agent/4; the key clauses follow. Module declarations
(exported/imported, #-qualified calls) are elided from quoted clauses.
Channels. A channel is an authenticated bidirectional communication structure ch(In, Out), where
In is a stream of incoming messages and Out is a stream of outgoing messages. Two agents share a
channel by holding opposite views of the same underlying stream pair: what one writes on its Out, the
other reads from its In. The procedure new_channel/2 creates such a pair:
new_channel(ch(Xs?, Ys), ch(Ys?, Xs)).
A single call new_channel(PQCh, QPCh) binds PQCh to ch(Xs?, Ys) and QPCh to ch(Ys?, Xs):
the reader Xs? of one channel is paired with the writer Xs of the other (and likewise for Ys/Ys?), so each
end’s outgoing stream is the other end’s incoming stream. Sending and receiving on a channel are:
send(X, ch(In, [X?|Out?]), ch(In?, Out)).
receive(X?, ch([X|In], Out?), ch(In?, Out)).
Channels are used in friend-mediated introduction (below) to establish a direct connection between two
parties without a cold-call. They are also used by the cold-call protocol: when the recipient accepts, it
returns a freshly-created setup-channel half as its response; the friend channel itself is conveyed over the
setup channel by the shared commit procedure befriend_commit, completing the bidirectional link.
Cold-call befriending. The user’s connect(Target) request triggers a cold-call: the agent sends
intro(Id, Resp) on the network and injects the paired reader Resp? into its own user-input stream,
so the eventual response surfaces locally as a normal user-side message:
agent(Id, [msg(person, Id1, connect(Target))|UserIn], NetIn, Outs) :-
      Id? =?= Id1?, ground(Target?) |
      send_net(msg(Target?, intro(Id?, Resp)), Outs?, Outs1),
      inject_msg(Resp?, Target?, Id?, UserIn?, UserIn1),
      agent(Id?, UserIn1?, NetIn?, Outs1?).
On the recipient side, an incoming intro(From, Resp?) captures the response writer Resp, forwarded
to the user in a befriend prompt; the user’s decision will assign it, transparently transported back to the
originator:

agent(Id, UserIn, [msg(Id1, intro(From, Resp?))|NetIn], Outs) :-
      Id? =?= Id1? |
      send_user(msg(agent, person, befriend(From?, Resp)), Outs?, Outs1),
      agent(Id?, UserIn?, NetIn?, Outs1?).
When the user makes a decision (accept or reject), the agent processes the response:
agent(Id, [msg(person, Id1,
      decision(Dec, From, response(Resp?)))|UserIn], NetIn, Outs) :-
      Id? =?= Id1? |
      bind_response(Id?, Dec?, From?, Resp, Outs?, Outs1, NetIn?, NetIn1),
      agent(Id?, UserIn?, NetIn1?, Outs1?).
The helper bind_response handles both cases: on yes, it creates a fresh setup-channel pair via
new_channel, assigns accept(RetCh?) to Resp (transported back to the initiator by the Communicate
transaction) and commits the friendship over the local half via the shared befriend_commit, which
conveys a fresh friend channel over the setup channel, registers its friend_output entry, and merges
its incoming end into NetIn; on no, it informs the user the request was rejected. Both ends run the same
idempotent commit. Its definition appears in Appendix F.
     When Alice executes this protocol to befriend Bob, her agent sends msg(bob, intro(alice, Resp))
on the net_output stream. The Cold-call transaction (Definition 3.6) transfers this message to Bob’s
network input stream, adding the writer Resp to Bob’s resolvent. When Bob accepts, assigning Resp
to accept(RetCh?) for a fresh setup channel, the Communicate transaction transfers this assignment
back to Alice; befriend_commit then installs the friend channel at both ends.
Friend-mediated introduction. Friend-mediated introduction creates a fresh setup-channel pair via
new_channel/2 and sends one half to each of the introduced parties; on mutual acceptance a direct
connection arises without a cold-call:
agent(Id, [msg(person, Id1, introduce(P, Q))|UserIn], NetIn, Outs) :-
      Id? =?= Id1?, ground(P?), ground(Q?), P? =?\= Q?,
      new_channel(PQCh, QPCh) |
      send_friend(P?, msg(Id?, P?, intro(Q?, QPCh?)), Outs?, Outs1),
      send_friend(Q?, msg(Id?, Q?, intro(P?, PQCh?)), Outs1?, Outs2),
      agent(Id?, UserIn?, NetIn?, Outs2?).
When Bob introduces Alice to Charlie, the setup-channel pair he creates contains readers that are trans-
ferred to Alice and Charlie via Communicate; each party accepts by acknowledging on the setup channel,
and on mutual acknowledgement both commit the friendship via befriend_commit — a direct connec-
tion without any Cold-call.
Text messaging. Once two agents are friends, messages flow directly along their shared channel via the
Communicate transaction, without cold-calls. The user’s send(Target, Text) request is dispatched
by send_friend on the friend_output entry registered under Target in Outs:
agent(Id, [msg(person, Id1, send(Target, Text))|UserIn], NetIn, Outs) :-
      Id? =?= Id1?, ground(Target?) |
      send_friend(Target?, msg(Id?, Target?, text(Text?)), Outs?, Outs1),
      agent(Id?, UserIn?, NetIn?, Outs1?).
On the other side, an incoming text(Text) from From is forwarded to the user as received(From, Text):

agent(Id, UserIn, [msg(From, Id1, text(Text))|NetIn], Outs) :-
    Id? =?= Id1? |
    send_user(msg(agent, person, received(From?, Text?)), Outs?, Outs1),
    agent(Id?, UserIn?, NetIn?, Outs1?).

Unfriending. Either friend may unfriend the other, and the operation is one-way: no acknowledgement
and no decline. The user’s unfriend(Target) request notifies the peer on the friend channel, removes
the friend_output entry, and tells the person the friend is gone:
agent(Id, [msg(person, Id1, unfriend(Target))|UserIn], NetIn, Outs) :-
     Id? =?= Id1?, ground(Target?) |
     send_friend(Target?, msg(Id?, Target?, unfriend), Outs?, Outs1),
     close_friend(Target?, Outs1?, Outs2),
     send_user(msg(agent, person, unfriended(Target?)), Outs2?, Outs3),
     agent(Id?, UserIn?, NetIn?, Outs3?).
The receiving side closes its own half on the incoming unfriend:
agent(Id, UserIn, [msg(From, Id1, unfriend)|NetIn], Outs) :-
     Id? =?= Id1?, ground(From?) |
     close_friend(From?, Outs?, Outs1),
     send_user(msg(agent, person, unfriended(From?)), Outs1?, Outs2),
     agent(Id?, UserIn?, NetIn?, Outs2?).
close_friend does nothing where the named party is not a friend, so both clauses are idempotent and
an unfriend from both sides at once closes each half once.

5    Multiagent GLP is Grassroots
This section establishes that maGLP is grassroots. We recall the notion of grassroots from [24]; the
formal definitions appear in Appendix C.
    Informally, a protocol is grassroots if two disjoint groups of agents can each operate independently—
their interleaved correct runs are correct runs of the combined system—yet the combined system offers
genuinely new behaviours that neither group could produce on its own. The first requirement is oblivi-
ousness; the second is interactivity.
    The Cold-call transaction (Definition 3.6) is the fundamental interactive transaction of maGLP. It
allows an agent p in one group to connect to an agent q in another, establishing shared variables that span
both groups—a behaviour that no interleaving of independent runs of the two groups could produce. An
agent p cold-calls q once q is named in its state, and p learns of an agent it has not met from the person,
by the Input transaction.
    An agent is a constant of GLP. A group of agents running on its own is one whose members know no
agent outside it.
Definition 5.1 (maGLP Protocol). Let M be a GLP program and, for a finite P ⊂ Π, let S(P) be the set
of asynchronous resolvents over M in which no agent outside P occurs. The maGLP protocol over M
assigns to each P the maGLP transition system over P and M (Definition 3.6) with its configurations
restricted to S(P)P .
14                                                                                 Grassroots Logic Programs


Definition 5.2 (Names No Agent, Uses Cold Calls). A GLP program M names no agent if no agent
occurs in M. It uses cold calls if for every local state s of an agent p reachable in a run of the maGLP
protocol over M and every agent q, some Input p from s writing a term in which q occurs, followed
by Reduce p and Communicate p to p transactions, reaches a local state in which Cold-call p to q is
enabled.
Theorem 5.3. The maGLP protocol over a program that names no agent and uses cold calls is grass-
roots.
Corollary 5.4. The GLP implementation of the grassroots social graph (Section 4) is grassroots.

6    Conclusion
While concurrent logic programming and its powerful programming techniques have been known for
four decades [39], adoption has been hampered by their inaccessibility to the average programmer. Con-
current logic programming requires a substantial shift from sequential procedural or functional thinking
to dataflow programming via partial bindings, with concurrent operational semantics in which commu-
nication occurs through binding paired logical variables which may contain further logic variables. Our
experience to date is that AI is highly effective and productive at programming from mathematical spec-
ifications when the target is a typed high-level language such as GLP, where types constrain the space
of legal programs and provide a checkable specification at the human-AI interface [50]. Moreover, our
preliminary experience suggests that the abstract nature of concurrent logic programming renders it a
more powerful and productive language than mainstream languages for collaborative human-AI abstract
specifications-based program development.
     Constitutional governance of grassroots communities and federations builds on Constitutional Con-
sensus [18, 23] and digital social contracts [5, 54], both realisable in GLP. The GLP runtime and example
programs are open-source at https://github.com/EShapiro2/GLP.

A     Logic Programs
We recall standard Logic Programs (LP) notions of syntax, most-general unifier (mgu), and semantics
via goal reduction.
Definition A.1 (Logic Programs Syntax). We employ standard LP notions. Let V denote the set of
variables (identifiers beginning with uppercase). A term is a variable, a constant (numbers, strings, or
the empty list []), or a compound term f (T1 , . . . , Tn ) with functor f and subterms Ti . Let T denote the
set of all terms. We use standard list notation: [X|Xs] for a list cell, [X1,...,Xn] for finite lists. A
term is ground if it contains no variables.
18                                                                               Grassroots Logic Programs


    A unit goal is a compound term or a string, also commonly referred to as an atom. A goal is a
multiset of unit goals; the empty goal is written true. A clause A :- B has head A (a unit goal) and body
B (a goal); a unit clause has empty body. A logic program is a finite set of clauses; clauses for the same
predicate form a procedure. Let G (P) denote the set of goals over the vocabulary of the program P.
    A substitution σ is an idempotent function σ : V → T , a mapping from variables to terms applied
to a fixed point. By convention, σ (x) = xσ . Let Σ denote the set of all substitutions. We assume standard
notions of instance, ground, renaming, renaming apart, unifier, and most-general unifier (mgu).
Definition A.2 (LP Goal/Clause Reduction). Given an LP unit goal A and clause C, with H :- B being
the result of renaming C apart from A, the LP reduction of A with C succeeds with (B, σ ) if A and H have
an mgu σ .
Definition A.3 (Logic Programs Transition System). A transition system LP(P) = (C, c0 , T ) is a Logic
Programs transition system for a logic program P and initial goal G0 ∈ G (P), if C = G (P) × Σ, c0 =
      / and T is the set of all transitions (G, σ ) → (G′ , σ ′ ) such that for some unit goal A ∈ G and clause
(G0 , 0),
C ∈ P the LP reduction of A with C succeeds with (B, σ̂ ), G′ = (G \ {A} ∪ B)σ̂ , and σ ′ = σ ◦ σ̂ .
    LP has two forms of nondeterminism: the choice of A ∈ G, called and-nondeterminism, and the
choice of C ∈ P, called or-nondeterminism, and as such are closely-related to Alternating Turing Ma-
chines [36].
Definition A.4 (Proper and Successful Run, Outcome). A run ρ : (G0 , σ0 ) → · · · → (Gn , σn ) of LP(P)
is proper if for any 1 ≤ i < n, a variable that occurs in Gi+1 but not in Gi also does not occur in any G j ,
 j < i. If proper, the outcome of ρ is (G0 :- Gn )σn . Such a run is successful if Gn = 0.
                                                                                        /
   The following proposition justifies the computation-as-deduction view of LP [20], calling a proper
LP run a derivation and a successful proper run a successful derivation.
Proposition A.5 (LP Computation is Deduction). The outcome (G0 :- Gn )σ of a proper run of LP(P)
is a logical consequence of P.
    The LP(P) transition system allows defining several denotational semantic notions for a program P:
(1) the clause semantics is the set of all outcomes of all proper runs with an initial most-general unit
goal (arguments are distinct variables), closely related to the fully-abstract compositional semantics of
LP [10]; (2) the atom semantics is the set of all outcomes of all successful derivations with an initial
most-general unit goal; (3) the ground atom semantics is the standard model-theoretic semantics, the set
of ground instances of the atom semantics over the Herbrand universe of P [27].

B    Term Matching
If two terms T1 and T2 that jointly satisfy SO are unifiable with an mgu σ , then σ maps any variable in
T1 to a subterm of T2 and vice versa. Hence, the SO invariant of GLP allows eschewing unification in
favour of term matching that performs joint term-tree traversal and collects variable assignments along
the way.
Definition B.1 (Term Matching). Given two terms T1 and T2 that jointly satisfy SO, their term matching
proceeds via the joint traversal of the term-trees of T1 and T2 , consulting the following table at each pair
of joint vertices, where X1 , X2 denote writers, X1 ?, X2 ? denote readers, and f /n denotes a non-variable
term, a constant when n = 0 and a compound term when n > 0:

                      T1 \T2             Writer X2    Reader X2 ?   Term f2 /n2
                      Writer X1          fail         X1 := X2 ?    X1 := T2

                      Term f1 /n1        X2 := T1     fail          fail if f1 ̸= f2 or n1 ̸= n2

C     Grassroots Definitions
This appendix recalls from [24] the definitions of protocols and of the grassroots property referenced in
Section 5, and the condition under which a protocol defined by transactions is oblivious, and extends the
latter to a protocol in which the person acts.
C.1    Protocols and the Grassroots Property
A protocol is a family of multiagent transition systems, one for each finite set of agents P ⊂ Π, sharing
an underlying set of local states with a designated initial state.
Definition C.1 (Local-States Function [24]). A local-states function S : 2Π → 2S maps every finite set
of agents P ⊂ Π to a set of local states S(P) ⊂ S that includes s0 and satisfies P ⊂ P′ =⇒ S(P) ⊂ S(P′ ).
    Given a local-states function S, we use C(P) := S(P)P for configurations over P and c0 (P) := {s0 }P
for the initial configuration.
Definition C.2 (Protocol [24]). A protocol F over a local-states function S is a family of multiagent
transition systems with exactly one transition system F (P) = (C(P), c0 (P), T (P), ∼ (P)) for every finite
P ⊂ Π.
    A correct run of a transition system is safe and live (Definition 2.5); the runs here are safe by con-
struction, so correctness coincides with liveness.
Lemma C.3 (Extension [24]). Every finite safe run of a transition system is a prefix of a correct run of
it.
    Informally, in a grassroots protocol two disjoint groups of agents can each operate independently—
their interleaved correct runs are correct runs of the combined system—yet the combined system offers
genuinely new behaviours that neither group could produce on its own. To define this formally, we first
define the interleaving of runs of two disjoint groups.
Definition C.4 (Interleaving [24]). Let P, P′ ⊂ Π be disjoint nonempty sets of agents, r = c0 , c1 , . . . a
run of F (P), and r′ = d0 , d1 , . . . a run of F (P′ ). An interleaving of r and r′ is a sequence e0 , e1 , . . . of
configurations in C(P ∪ P′ ) for which there exist non-decreasing sequences of indices (ik )k≥0 and ( jk )k≥0
with i0 = j0 = 0 such that for every k ≥ 0:
1. (ek ) p = (cik ) p for every p ∈ P,
2. (ek )q = (d jk )q for every q ∈ P′ ,
3. if ek+1 exists, then exactly one of: (a) ik+1 = ik + 1 and jk+1 = jk (a P-step), or (b) ik+1 = ik and
    jk+1 = jk + 1 (a P′ -step).
Moreover, if r is finite of length n then ik = n for some k, and if r is infinite then for every m ≥ 0 there is
a k with ik = m; likewise for r′ and ( jk ).
20                                                                                     Grassroots Logic Programs


    Note that an interleaving is well-defined: since P ⊂ P ∪ P′ , the local-states function ensures S(P) ⊂
S(P ∪ P′ ), and similarly for P′ , so each ek is a valid configuration in C(P ∪ P′ ). Also, e0 = c0 (P ∪ P′ ),
since (e0 ) p = (c0 ) p = s0 for all p ∈ P and (e0 )q = (d0 )q = s0 for all q ∈ P′ .
Definition C.5 (Interaction, Interactive Run [24]). Let Q ⊂ Π. For a configuration c over Q and R ⊆ Q,
write c|R for the restriction of c to R. A transition e → e′ of F (Q) is an interaction between x ̸= y ∈ Q
if e|{x} ̸= e′ |{x} and e|{y} ̸= e′ |{y} , and e|{x} → e′ |{x} is not a transition of F ({x}) or e|{y} → e′ |{y} is not
a transition of F ({y}). It is an interaction between disjoint nonempty P, P′ ⊆ Q if it is an interaction
between some x ∈ P and some y ∈ P′ . A run r̂ of F (Q) is interactive between P and P′ if some transition
of r̂ is an interaction between them.
Definition C.6 (Oblivious, Interactive, Grassroots [24]). A protocol F is:
1. oblivious if for every disjoint nonempty P, P′ ⊂ Π, every interleaving of a correct run of F (P) and a
   correct run of F (P′ ) is a correct run of F (P ∪ P′ ).
2. interactive if for every Q ⊂ Π and disjoint nonempty P, P′ ⊆ Q, every prefix of a correct run of F (Q)
   containing no interaction between P and P′ is a prefix of a correct run of F (Q) that contains one.
3. grassroots if it is oblivious and interactive.
    Being oblivious means that two disjoint groups of agents, each running the protocol independently
and correctly, do not interfere with each other: any interleaving of their independent correct runs is a
correct run of the combined system. Being interactive means that two disjoint groups that have not
interacted can still do so: the combined system has a correct continuation in which one transition engages
an agent of each group. In an interleaving, each step changes the local states of agents in only one group;
therefore, any transaction whose active participants span both groups yields a step that cannot occur in
any interleaving. These definitions are the machine-layer specialisation, without guards, of the volitional
grassroots framework of [24].
C.2    Transactions-Based Protocols
Definition C.7 (Transactions Over a Local-States Function [24]). Let S be a local-states function. A set
of transactions R is over S if every transaction t ∈ R is a transition over participants Q and local states
S(Q′ ) for some Q ⊆ Q′ ⊂ Π. Given such R and P ⊂ Π:
               R(P) := {t ∈ R : t has participants Q ⊆ P and is over S(Q′ ) for some Q′ ⊆ P}
    A transaction of agents of P from local states of P may reach a local state of a larger set of agents,
as when p adds to its state an agent outside P. Such a transaction is in R(P ∪ P′ ) and not in R(P), so an
interleaving of correct runs of F (P) and F (P′ ) may owe it a liveness obligation that the run of F (P)
does not owe [24]. It is excluded as follows.
Definition C.8 (Contained [24]). A set of transactions R over a local-states function S is contained if for
every P ⊂ Π, every t = c → c′ in R with participants Q ⊆ P and c ∈ S(P)Q is in R(P).
Definition C.9 (Transactions-Based Protocol [24]). Let S be a local-states function and R a set of trans-
actions over S, in which distinct transactions have disjoint P-closures for every P ⊂ Π, with a partial
equivalence ∼. The protocol F over R, S, and ∼ is defined by F (P) := (C(P), c0 (P), R(P)↑P, ∼↑P) for
each P ⊂ Π, where R(P)↑P and ∼↑P are the P-closures (Definition 3.3) of R(P) and of ∼ restricted to
R(P).
    Since liveness applies to all classes, any class [t] in R(P ∪ P′ )/∼ whose transactions have participants
spanning both P and P′ could obstruct obliviousness if enabled in an interleaving; so could a class whose
transactions have participants in P and that is not a class of F (P), which containment excludes.
Shapiro                                                                                                  21


Proposition C.10 (Obliviousness Condition [24]). A transactions-based protocol over a contained set of
transactions is oblivious provided that for every disjoint nonempty P, P′ ⊂ Π, no class whose transactions
have participants spanning both P and P′ is ever enabled in any interleaving of correct runs of F (P)
and F (P′ ).
   In maGLP the person acts: the Input transaction (Definition 3.6) is the person’s, belongs to no class,
and may bring into an agent’s state the name of an agent outside its group. It is the machine-layer
counterpart of a volition change in [24], and is added to a transactions-based protocol as follows.
Definition C.11 (Transactions-Based Protocol with Input). Let F be the protocol over R, S, and ∼, and
I a set of unary transactions over S, each t ∈ I over one participant p and S(Q′ ) for some Q′ ∋ p. The
protocol over R, S, ∼ and input I is F I with F I (P) := (C(P), c0 (P), R(P)↑P ∪ I(P)↑P, ∼↑P) for each
P ⊂ Π; a transition of I(P)↑P is in no class.

Proposition C.12 (Obliviousness Condition with Input). A transactions-based protocol with input over
a contained set of transactions is oblivious provided that for every disjoint nonempty P, P′ ⊂ Π, no class
whose transactions have participants spanning both P and P′ is ever enabled in any interleaving of
correct runs of F I (P) and F I (P′ ).

Theorem C.13 (Transactions-Based Grassroots). A transactions-based protocol with input that satisfies
the condition of Proposition C.12 and is interactive is grassroots.

Predefined types. The predefined types, available in every GLP program, are Stream(X),

types built into the runtime. An Integer is a 64-bit two’s-complement integer and a Real an IEEE 754

Dart, and its integers are Dart’s. A number is written as its digits, with an optional minus directly before

white space or a line break there. Opaque, the union of all opaque types, is built into the type checker: a
program can only pass a term of it on (Typed GLP companion paper [50]). Term, the type of every term,
is built into the type checker likewise (Typed GLP companion paper).

Kernel and seam predicates. A predicate defined by GLP clauses whose body may invoke a body
kernel is of one of two kinds, and the difference is what lies on the other side of it. A kernel predicate
reaches the runtime that implements GLP: arithmetic, term structure, the clock, the module system. A
seam predicate reaches something the language cannot reach at all — the person, the person’s keys, the
networking layer, the platform. The first kind is GLP realised; the second is GLP joined to what is not
GLP, and a seam is where two things are joined.

     The distinction is not one of mechanism: both are ordinary GLP clauses, both may name a body
kernel in call position, and both are admitted only to a system module. It decides where the specification
of a construct’s behaviour belongs. A kernel predicate’s behaviour is the language’s own and is stated
here. A seam predicate’s is not: what an address is, what a geofence reports, what a signature attests are
settled by the paper that specifies the thing on the far side, and this catalogue states the predicate and
cites that paper.

    Both tables list those in root scope, where every program reaches them; a system module may define
others for its own use, and those belong to that module rather than to the language.

    A program declares every procedure it defines, with no _ or _? in a declaration or in a type definition;
the two stand only in a signature of these tables that takes any term, as run/2 and ..= do.

Term composition and decomposition (=.., ..=). Composition and decomposition are two predicates,
not one bidirectional predicate. A declaration fixes one mode per argument, so a single name cannot
carry both directions: =.. takes a list and produces a term, and ..= takes a term and produces a list,
each with the opposite mode on both arguments. T =.. [foo, a, b]? constructs T = foo(a, b);
L ..= foo(a, b)? produces L = [foo, a, b].
    Both are needed, and a program that walks an arbitrary term needs both together: ..= to take a
compound apart and recurse on its arguments, =.. to rebuild it from the results. Decomposition alone
descends without returning, and composition alone cannot get started.
Clock access (now/1). Binds the argument to the current Unix timestamp in milliseconds since the
epoch.
Arithmetic evaluation and assignment (:=). Arithmetic assignment is defined recursively: each clause
pattern-matches on an arithmetic operator, guards verify that operands are of the required type, and the
body invokes the corresponding body kernel. An otherwise clause handles the case where operands
are themselves expressions, evaluating them recursively before retrying.
Seeded random numbers (random/4). random(Seed, N, Value, NextSeed) binds Value to a
pseudo-random integer with 1 ≤ Value ≤ N, and NextSeed to the seed of the next draw, given a
ground seed Seed and a ground bound N ≥ 1. It is a body goal, not a guard; the clause is guarded by
integer(Seed?), integer(N?), N? >= 1 and invokes the ’_random’ body kernel. NextSeed is
determined by Seed alone; Value by Seed and N. The seed stream is therefore independent of the bound
requested, and a computation that threads NextSeed from each draw into the next reproduces exactly
from its initial seed. The generator is SplitMix64 [17], with its root increment and its mixing function, in
64-bit arithmetic modulo 264 : a seed is a 64-bit integer, and every 64-bit integer is a seed; NextSeed is
Seed plus the increment 0x9E3779B97F4A7C15; and Value is one plus the mix of NextSeed, taken as
an unsigned 64-bit integer, modulo N, where the mix of z is z ← (z ⊕ (z ≫ 33)) · 0xFF51AFD7ED558CCD,
then z ← (z ⊕ (z ≫ 33)) · 0xC4CEB9FE1A85EC53, then z ⊕ (z ≫ 33), with ≫ the unsigned shift.
Splitting a seed (split random seed/3). split_random_seed(Seed, S1, S2) assigns S1 and S2
two seeds determined by Seed, such that the sequences of draws from Seed, from S1 and from S2 are
independent streams: S1 is the mix of Seed plus the increment, and S2 the mix of Seed plus twice the
increment, the increment and the mix those of random/4. It is a body goal, not a guard; the clause is
guarded by integer(Seed?) and invokes the ’_split_random_seed’ body kernel. A computation
that draws from several seeds made by splitting one seed reproduces exactly from that seed.

Simulated time (start clock/1, rated/2, step clock/2). A machine has a simulated clock,
a real number of seconds, and a queue of pending tokens, each with an activation time; now/1
and the time guards read the system clock. The types are Token ::= go(Number) ; stop and
ClockStep ::= released(Number) ; stopped(Number).                    start_clock(Seed) binds every
pending token to stop, sets the clock to 0, and seeds the clock’s generator with Seed; the clause is
guarded by integer(Seed?). rated(Rate, Tok) makes the token Tok pending, with activation time
the clock plus D, where D is drawn from the exponential distribution with rate Rate per second; the
clause is guarded by number(Rate?), Rate? > 0. step_clock(Horizon, Step) takes one step of
the clock, and the clause is guarded by number(Horizon?). Where a token is pending whose activation
time T is the least and at most Horizon, the step sets the clock to T , binds that token to go(T ) and
assigns Step the term released(T ); two tokens of one activation time are released in the order in
which they were made pending. Otherwise the step binds every pending token to stop and assigns Step
the term stopped(C), C being the clock. Each of the three is a body goal that invokes the body kernel
of its name, and rated and step_clock abort before the first start_clock.
     The clock’s generator is that of random/4, its state a seed. A draw replaces the state by the state
plus the increment, and D = − ln(U)/Rate, where U is one plus the mix of the new state, taken as an
unsigned 64-bit integer and shifted right by 11 bits, over 253 , so that 0 < U ≤ 1. A computation that
makes its tokens pending in the same order reproduces exactly from the seed.
     A program takes a step in the body of a clause guarded by when_idle: the guard tests that the ma-
chine has no Reduce or Communicate to make, and the step is taken after it, a guard changing nothing. So
every goal has been reduced as far as it can be at the present simulated time before the clock moves, and
a goal waiting on a token is reduced once its time has come. The clock is of one machine, as when_idle
is. The three predicates implement the rated goal and the Release transition of Stochastic GLP [49]: the
queue, the draws and the clock are the engine’s, as they were the emulator’s in the implementation of the
stochastic pi-calculus in Flat Concurrent Prolog [31], by Gillespie’s method [11].
Output to the person (send to user/1). send_to_user(Stream) emits each element of Stream,
a stream of any one type, to the person operating the agent, in order, and ends when the stream ends.
Its clause is guarded by ground on the element and invokes the ’_output’ body kernel. A module
reaches the person through this predicate; naming ’_output’ in call position is admitted only to a
system module. Where the person is a terminal, each element is printed on a line of its own, a string as
its characters.
Text (string concat/2, term to string/2). string_concat(Ss, S) assigns S the string of the
characters of the strings of the list Ss, one after another; the empty list contributes the characters [].
Its clause is guarded by ground(Ss?) and invokes the ’_string_concat’ body kernel, which aborts
where an element is not a string. term_to_string(T, S) assigns S a text that the reader reads as the
ground term T: a string the reader would read unquoted as anything else in single quotes, a number as
the reader reads it back, a structure as its functor so written, an opening parenthesis, its arguments and a
closing parenthesis, and a list as [a, b] or [a, b | T], arguments and elements separated by a comma
and a space, so that a term has one text at every agent. Its clause is guarded by ground(T?) and invokes
the ’_term_to_string’ body kernel. A program that prints GLP source, as a compiler does, builds
each line as a string by these two, a constant of the source by term_to_string/2 and a variable or a
type T? by string_concat/2, and prints it by send_to_user/1.

Module as a value (self module/1, decompose module/4). self_module(Module) binds its argu-
ment to the caller’s own module, a value of the primitive type Module. decompose_module(Module, Key, Source, Com
takes a module value apart, assigning the public key of the compiler that produced it, the identity of the
source it was compiled from, and the identity of the compiled module itself.
    The two identities are distinct and neither determines the other. Source identity says what was writ-
ten; compiled identity says what runs, and two compilers compiling one source produce two of them.
The compiler’s key is what joins them: it is the compiler that vouches for the correspondence, and a
party relying on the pair decides for itself whether that key is one it accepts. A module value is opaque
to a program, so the decomposition has to come from the runtime. Neither identity is the type identity of
run/3 and find_type/2, which is a property of a declaration and not of a module.
Dynamic activation (run/2, run/3, find type/2, find type/3, opaque type/3). run(Goal, Module)
activates the module value Module and posts Goal to it; the predicate of Goal must be exported by
Module, else an error. The readers and writers of Goal connect the caller and the activated module.
run(Goal, Type, Module) activates Module and posts Goal to it where Type equals the type identity
that Module’s exports record for Goal’s predicate, and errs otherwise. find_type(P/N, Type)
assigns Type the type identity of the declaration of P/N in the caller’s scope, and error where
P/N is not declared there. Type identity is defined in the Typed GLP companion paper [50], which
also gives its construction and proves the compatibility it rests on. find_type(P/N, Hs, Type)
assigns Type the type identity of the opaque instance at Hs of the caller’s parameterised declaration
of P/N, Hs a list of type identities, one per type parameter of the declaration in declaration order,
and errs where P/N is not declared there or the length of Hs is not the number of its type parameters.
opaque_type(P/N, Module, Hs) assigns Hs the list of the type identities of the types at which
Module’s exports record its entry P/N as the instance of a parameterised declaration, in the order of
its parameters, and errs where they record none. A host activates a guest it was not compiled with by
opaque_type(P/N, M, Hs), find_type(P/N, Hs, Type) and run(Goal, Type, M), holding no
declaration of the guest’s own types. At a position whose type has a parameter of the template in it,
Goal may hold a variable whose type has Opaque in its place, at which a host holds the channel ends
of its guests. The opaque instance, Opaque, the invitation discipline under which this is sound, and its
proof are in the Typed GLP companion paper.

Identity and signature (self key/1, sign/3, signature/2). The runtime makes the agent’s key pair
when it spawns the agent, before anything is compiled or sent, from a seed drawn from outside the pro-
gram, as the companion implementation paper says, and gives it to the networking layer, which holds
the private key and signs with it [2]. A Key is the 64-character lowercase hexadecimal of the 32-byte
Ed25519 public key, so @< orders keys as their bytes. self_key(Key) assigns Key the agent’s pub-
lic key. sign(Term, Key, Signed) requires Term ground and Key a key of that person, and assigns
Signed the signature under Key of the pair of Term and the source identity of the module making the
call; Key is the agent’s own, the layer holding no other, and any other key aborts the call. A signed term
is a string, so it travels as text, as an invitation link does [2]. signature(Signed, Signature) takes
a signed term apart and reports what it finds. Where Signed is a signed term whose signature verifies,
it assigns Signature the term signed(Key, Hash, Term), giving the signer’s key, the identity of
the module under which the signature was made, and the term signed; otherwise it assigns the constant
unsigned. The type is Signature ::= signed(Key, Hash, _) ; unsigned, and the caller dis-
criminates by matching, taking signed(Key, _, _) where it needs to know who signed and not what.
A predicate that did not hold on a forgery could not refuse one and carry on, which is what an agent
presented with an invitation, an undertaking or a creator’s certification does.
    The module identity is the runtime’s contribution and cannot be the program’s, because a program
can build any term it likes. Were the signature over Term alone, any module the person runs could obtain
that person’s signature on any statement whatever, and a signature would attest only that the person ran
something. Scoped, it says that the person ran this module and that this module asked for this term,
which is what a relying party needs.
    A signature is what carries a claim to a party that has neither an authenticated channel to its author
nor an attestation of the runtime that produced it. Within a conversation none is needed: authorship of
every stream extension is already established to its reader by the channel and by the attested runtime at
the writer’s end. It is needed outside one — a rendezvous agent belongs to no social graph and is itself
unattested, and admits a connecting agent on a signed capability and on nothing else.

agent at its other end is not the one with which the name was exchanged; while it is held the runtime
places nothing on its channel and applies nothing arriving on it. The decision is the program’s rather
than the runtime’s because the reason to release or drop such a link is not visible to the runtime, which
therefore reports and waits. The mechanism that holds a link and reports it is described in the companion

device’s location would let the same local state reduce differently at different times. A declaration carries
a name and a radius and returns no coordinates, so a program learns of its own device’s location only that
it entered or left a place it named itself. The runtime behind the kernels they invoke is described in the
companion implementation paper [48]; the layer beneath that is specified in the networking companion
paper.
Proximity trust. A proximity underlay carries a cold-call trust level, which decides whether the agent
contacts, and answers, a device it does not already know [2]. trust(U, L) declares that level for
one underlay: U is pan (Bluetooth Low Energy) or lan (the local network) and L is open or closed.
Both underlays stand at closed until declared, a later declaration of an underlay replaces the level then
standing, and the two are declared independently, so an agent may answer strangers over one and ignore
them over the other.
    The level is the program’s to set and cannot be the layer’s, for a reason that decides where the
predicate belongs: an agent raises the level on entering a place it trusts and lowers it on leaving, and
lowers it again when that place’s crossings become unobservable, doubt resolving to closed — and the
layer cannot do any of that, because it does not know which place is trusted. The program declared the
place.

Multiway merge (mwm/2). mwm(In, Out) merges the streams named by In into the single stream Out,
preserving the order of each input stream and interleaving them fairly. In is a stream of merge(S)
elements, each contributing the stream S to the merge, following the merger of FCP [39]. Since In is
itself a stream, a merge(S) element arriving later grafts a further stream into a running merge: the set of
merged streams need not be known when the merge begins, and a merge is closed by closing In.
     mwm/2 is the construct to use whenever many streams are merged into one, and is preferred to a tree
of merge/3 goals for that purpose. The difference is asymptotic, not stylistic. Merging n streams with
binary merge/3 passes each message through log n merge processes on a balanced tree, and through
as many as n on the linear tree a program most easily writes; mwm/2 appends each message once, in
constant time, through a handle on the tail of Out. A program that builds its own merge tree pays a factor
no implementation can recover, since the cost is in the process structure the program chose. None of this
makes a hand-built merge tree wrong: it is sound, and knowing how to build one is worth having. The
preference is about which construct a program should reach for when many streams must arrive at one
receiver, not about which technique is worth understanding.
     merge/3 remains the right construct where two streams are merged, or where the merge tree itself
carries meaning — a merge goal that is also the point at which a process terminates. mwm/2 is for
many-to-one merging, which is the common case in a program routing many senders to one receiver.

Proposition E.1 (cGLP Computation is Deduction). Let (G0 :- Gn )σn⋆ be the outcome of a proper cGLP
run ρ : (G0 , σ0 ) → · · · → (Gn , σn ). Then (G0 :- Gn )σn⋆ /? is a logical consequence of P/?.

Proof. The /? operator replaces every reader X? by its paired writer X, transforming GLP terms into LP
terms. We show that the cGLP run ρ corresponds to an LP run ρ/? of LP(P/?).
    Consider a cGLP transition (G, σ ) → (G′ , σ ′ ):
• Reduce transition: Goal A reduces with clause C via writer mgu σ̂ , and (σ̂ ?)⋆ is applied to the body.
  Applying /?, the clause C/? is an LP clause, and A/? unifies with the head H/? via the mgu σ̂ /? =
  (σ̂ ?)⋆ /? (since writers map to writers), which LP applies to the body. This is a valid LP reduction.
• Communicate transition: A reader X? ∈ G is replaced by the value T assigned to its paired writer.
  Under /?, both X? and X map to X, so this transition becomes the identity—the variable X is already
  assigned T in the LP view.
    Thus each cGLP transition corresponds to zero or one LP transitions, and the cGLP run ρ projects to
an LP run ρ/? of LP(P/?). By standard LP soundness, the outcome of ρ/? is a logical consequence of
P/?.

Proposition E.2 (SO Preservation). If the initial goal G0 satisfies SO, then every goal in a proper cGLP
run satisfies SO.

Proof. By induction on the length of the run. The base case is immediate: G0 satisfies SO by assumption.

Proposition E.3 (Monotonicity). In any proper cGLP run, if unit goal A can reduce with clause C at
step i, then either an instance of A has been reduced by step j > i, or an instance of A can still reduce
with C at step j.

Proof. Suppose goal A can reduce with clause C at step i, meaning the writer mgu of A and the head H
of (a renaming of) C succeeds. Consider what can change between steps i and j > i:
• Reduce transitions on other goals: These do not affect A directly. By SO, no other goal shares a writer
  with A, so no other reduction can assign a writer in A.
• Communicate transitions: These instantiate readers, not writers. A Communicate transition X? := T
  may instantiate a reader X? ∈ A, yielding A′ = A{X? := T }. We show A′ can still reduce with C:
  The original writer mgu succeeded, meaning at position p where X? occurred in A, H had a writer Y
  at position p, yielding assignment Y := X?; a reader or a non-variable term of H at position p would
  have caused failure or suspension, respectively, contradicting the success of the writer mgu.
  After the Communicate transition, A′ has T at position p. The clause C (renamed apart from A′ ) has a
  fresh writer Y ′ at position p. The writer mgu now yields Y ′ := T , which succeeds.
• Reduce transition on A: If A itself is reduced at some step k with i < k ≤ j, then an instance of A has
  been reduced, satisfying the proposition.
    Thus, if A has not been reduced by step j, the (possibly instantiated) goal A′ at step j can still reduce
with a fresh renaming of C.

Proposition E.5 (maGLP SO Preservation). If the initial goals of all agents satisfy SO, then every goal
in every agent’s resolvent throughout a proper run satisfies SO.

Proof. Reduce p preserves SO at agent p by the same argument as cGLP SO Preservation (Proposi-

F.1   Types and Channels
The protocol type vocabulary — Response, Decision, NetColdCall, FriendContent, IntroContent,
OutputEntry, OutputsList, and the rest — is defined in self.glp of the repository directory (Sec-
tion 4).
    A Channel is a pair of streams for bidirectional communication. The new_channel guard, shown
in Section 4, creates complementary channel endpoints: each reads from its first stream and writes to its
second, and each end’s outgoing stream is the other end’s incoming stream.
F.2   Agent Structure
Each agent processes messages from two separate input streams—user and network—and maintains
a typed outputs list (Section 4). The four arguments of agent/4 are: agent identity, user input
38                                                                              Grassroots Logic Programs


stream, network input stream, and the outputs list. The outputs list initially contains two entries—
user_output(...) and net_output(...)—providing streams to the user interface and the network,
respectively. As the agent befriends others, friend_output entries are added.

F.3   Cold-Call Befriending Protocol
The cold-call protocol enables agents to establish friendship without prior shared variables. Its initiating,
receiving, and decision clauses appear in Section 4; the remaining clauses, among them the two that
handle the response to a sent cold-call, are in agent.glp of the repository directory.
    The protocol works as follows: (1) Alice sends connect(bob) to her agent via the person stream;
(2) her agent sends a 2-argument msg(bob, intro(alice, Resp)) via the net_output entry, includ-
ing a fresh response variable Resp; (3) Bob receives msg(bob, intro(alice, Resp)) on his network
input and forwards the request to his user interface; (4) Bob decides yes or no; (5) Bob’s agent creates a
setup-channel pair and sends accept(RetCh?) back via Resp; (6) Alice’s agent receives the response,
and both agents commit the friendship via the shared befriend_commit, which conveys a fresh friend
channel over the setup channel and adds a friend_output entry on each side.

F.4   Channel Establishment
When a cold-call is accepted, both agents establish symmetric channels; the procedures bind_response
and handle_response are in agent.glp of the repository directory.
    The accepting agent creates a setup-channel pair via new_channel(RetCh, LocalCh),
sends RetCh back to the initiator, and commits over LocalCh. The shared library procedure
befriend_commit creates the fresh friend channel, conveys it as friend_channel(...) over the
setup channel, adds the friend_output entry, and merges the friend’s incoming stream into the agent’s
network input; it is idempotent, so both ends run the same commit.

F.5   Text Messaging
Once agents are friends, they can exchange text messages; the sending and receiving clauses appear in
Section 4.

F.6   Friend-Mediated Introduction
Once agents are friends, they can introduce each other to third parties. The introducing clause, which
creates a fresh channel pair and sends each half to the respective parties, appears in Section 4; the
receiving-side clauses are in agent.glp of the repository directory, and the procedures they call,
intro_await_peer and inject_intro_result, are in routing/intro.glp of the repository
directory.
    When Bob types introduce(alice, charlie), he creates a setup-channel pair via new_channel(PQCh, QPCh);
Alice and Charlie each receive one half. Accepting sends ack on the setup channel; intro_await_peer
waits for the peer’s ack (or nack) and injects the result, and on mutual acknowledgement each side
commits via befriend_commit. When both accept, they become direct friends without Bob’s further
involvement.
     Note the distinction between cold-call messages and friend-mediated messages: cold-call uses a 2-
argument msg(Target, Content) sent via the net_output entry, while friend-mediated introduction
uses a 3-argument msg(From, To, intro(Other, Ch)) sent directly to a friend via their named out-
put.

F.8   The Scenario and Actors
The complete scenario demonstrates three of the four protocols of Section 4; unfriending is not among
them:
1. Alice cold-calls Bob (Bob accepts) — Alice and Bob become friends
2. Alice sends Bob: “Hi Bob, this is Alice”
3. Bob cold-calls Charlie (Charlie accepts) — Bob and Charlie become friends
4. Charlie sends Bob: “Hi Bob, this is Charlie”
5. Bob introduces Alice to Charlie (both accept) — Alice and Charlie become direct friends
6. Alice sends Charlie: “Hi Charlie, this is Alice”
7. Charlie responds: “Hi Alice, this is Charlie”
